# What Happens When a Blue Whale Notices You

Draft 1. Target length: about 10 minutes (roughly 1,450 words at a calm narration pace).
Thumbnail text: IT FEELS YOU

Each scene has narration (what the voice says) and a visual note (what the animation shows).
Lines marked [CHECK] rest on commonly quoted figures that still need a primary source before publishing.

---

## 1. Hook

**Visual:** Black water. A tiny diver with a torch. Something enormous slides past behind them, far too big to fit on screen.

You are forty metres down, in open water, and there is nothing around you. No reef. No seabed. Just blue in every direction.

Then the blue behind you gets darker.

It isn't a shadow. It's an animal. And it keeps going. Ten metres. Twenty. Thirty. By the time its tail passes you, almost half a minute has gone by.

You have just met the largest animal that has ever lived. Not the largest alive today. The largest ever. Bigger than any dinosaur we have found.

So what happens next? Does it attack? Does it swallow you? Does it even know you are there?

The answer is stranger than any of those.

## 2. Scale

**Visual:** Side-by-side size comparisons that keep zooming out: diver, bus, whale. Then the whale next to a passenger jet.

Start with the size, because your brain will refuse it.

A big blue whale is about thirty metres long. That is three buses parked end to end. It can weigh more than one hundred and fifty tonnes, about the same as twenty-five elephants.

Its tongue alone weighs as much as one of those elephants. Its tail is as wide as a small aircraft's wing. When it breathes out, the spray climbs as high as a three-storey building.

And you are a little under two metres of soft tissue holding a torch.

## 3. The heart

**Visual:** Cutaway of the whale. The heart glows red and pulses very slowly. A beat counter on screen ticks: 2 per minute.

Inside that body is a heart the size of a small car. It weighs around one hundred and eighty kilograms.

You may have heard that a person could swim through its arteries. That one is a myth. The main artery is about as wide as a dinner plate. Big, but you are not getting through it.

The real number is better. In 2019, scientists stuck a heart monitor on a wild blue whale for the first time. When the whale dived, its heart slowed to as little as two beats per minute.

Two. One beat, then a long, long silence, then another.

Back at the surface, it raced up to more than thirty beats a minute to reload with oxygen. Researchers think this heart is working close to the limit of what a heart can do. Which may be why no animal has ever grown any bigger.

## 4. The mouth

**Visual:** The whale accelerates at a red cloud of krill. The mouth opens almost ninety degrees and the throat balloons out. Then a diagram of the throat with a grapefruit for scale.

Now the part you are actually worried about.

A blue whale feeds by lunging. It speeds up, drops its jaw open to almost ninety degrees, and takes in a mouthful of sea that can weigh as much as the whale itself. The pleated skin under its throat stretches like a parachute to hold it.

Then it pushes the water back out through hundreds of bristly plates and swallows what is left. Krill. Tiny, shrimp-like animals, each about the length of your finger.

A blue whale off California eats around sixteen tonnes of them in a single day.

So could it swallow you?

No. For all the size of that mouth, the throat behind it is narrow. Nothing much bigger than a grapefruit is going down. [CHECK]

If you were ever caught in a lunge, the whale would not eat you. It would close its mouth, feel something wrong, and spit you out. The danger is not being swallowed. The danger is the hit: a hundred and fifty tonnes of animal moving at speed, and a jaw closing with the weight of the sea behind it.

## 5. The sound

**Visual:** Rings of sound spread from the whale across a map of an ocean basin, crossing hundreds of kilometres. The diver's chest ripples as a ring passes.

Then there is the voice.

A blue whale's call is one of the loudest sounds made by any animal. It has been measured at up to one hundred and eighty-eight decibels in water. [CHECK]

But you probably would not hear it. Most of the call is pitched so low that it sits below the range of human hearing.

You would feel it instead. A pressure in your chest. A hum in your bones.

And that sound is not for you. It is a long-distance message. In the right conditions, a call like that can travel hundreds of kilometres through the ocean, to another whale you will never see.

## 6. Does it notice you?

**Visual:** The whale's eye, small on the huge head, slides past the diver. The diver is drawn as a tiny dot on the whale's "radar", then fades out.

So here is the question in the title. Does a blue whale notice you?

Probably, yes. Its eye is about the size of a grapefruit, and its hearing is tuned to the open ocean. A noisy, bubbling diver is not hard to detect.

What it does next is the surprising part.

Nothing.

There is no reliable record of a blue whale deliberately attacking a person. It has no teeth. It does not hunt anything bigger than krill. Most blue whales simply angle away from divers and boats and keep swimming.

You are not prey. You are not a threat. You are not even interesting.

And that is its own kind of frightening. You are sharing the water with something that could end you by accident, and it is not thinking about you at all.

## 7. The real danger

**Visual:** The tail sweeps down in slow motion. The diver tumbles in the wake. Then a ship's hull crossing above the whale.

Because the accident is the real risk.

One beat of that tail moves an enormous mass of water. Get too close behind it and you are thrown around like a leaf. Be in the wrong place when it surfaces, and a small boat simply goes over.

This is why countries that allow whale watching set minimum distances. Not because the whale is aggressive. Because it is thirty metres long and cannot see what is directly under its chin.

The same blind physics works against the whale. The biggest killer of blue whales today is not a predator. It is collisions with cargo ships.

## 8. What hunts it

**Visual:** A pod of orcas circling a blue whale from below, drawn small against it but many.

You would think nothing hunts an animal this size. For a long time, scientists thought so too.

Then, off Western Australia in 2019, researchers watched a pack of orcas chase down a blue whale, exhaust it, and kill it. It was the first time anyone had recorded it. They have seen it happen again since.

Even the largest animal on Earth has something to fear.

## 9. What nearly erased it

**Visual:** A population counter running down fast across the twentieth century, then creeping back up. Whaling ships as dark silhouettes.

But orcas were never the real problem. We were.

In the twentieth century, industrial whaling killed hundreds of thousands of blue whales. [CHECK] By the time hunting them was banned in the 1960s, a species that had filled the oceans for millions of years was almost gone.

They are recovering, slowly. Today there are thought to be somewhere between ten and twenty-five thousand. [CHECK] A fraction of what there once was.

## 10. Close

**Visual:** Back to the diver. The whale's tail disappears into the blue. The water goes quiet.

So what happens when a blue whale notices you?

It looks. It decides you do not matter. And it leaves.

The tail fades into the blue. The pressure in your chest eases. The water is empty again.

You were never in danger from its appetite. Only from its size.

And somewhere, hundreds of kilometres away, another whale just heard it pass.
