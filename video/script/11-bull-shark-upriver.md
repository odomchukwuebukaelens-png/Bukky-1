# What Happens When a Bull Shark Follows You Upriver

## 1. Cold open

**Visual:** Real footage: a wide brown river through forest, far from any coast. Animation: you wading waist-deep; under the surface, a grey shape turning towards your legs. Map insert: the river mouth hundreds of kilometres away. Title. The three promises.

You are standing in a river.

The water is warm and brown. You cannot see your own feet. The sea is hundreds of kilometres away.

This should be the safest place in the world from sharks.

But a few metres away, something heavy turns in the murk. It came in from the ocean, weeks ago, and swam upstream. Past towns. Past bridges. All the way to you.

It is a bull shark. And it is one of the very few sharks on Earth that can live in fresh water.

So what happens when a bull shark follows you upriver?

On the way, you will find out how a sea shark survives in fresh water. How it finds you in water you cannot see through. And the farthest from the sea a bull shark has ever been found.

## 2. The shark that does not belong here

**Visual:** Real photos: a bull shark close up (iNaturalist), its short blunt snout, small eyes, thick body. Animation: a bull shark beside a great white and a reef shark; the bull is shorter but heavier. Number card: UP TO ≈ 3.5 M.

Bull sharks are not the biggest sharks. Most adults are two to three metres long. Big females can grow to around three and a half.

But they are built heavy. A short, wide, blunt snout. Small eyes. A thick body, like a boxer.

They are named for that snout, and for the way they head-butt their prey before they bite.

Around the world, they go by many names. In southern Africa, people call it the Zambezi shark, after the great river it swims up. In Central America, it was once called the Lake Nicaragua shark.

They live along warm coasts all around the world. In bays, harbours, lagoons and river mouths. Places where people swim.

And unlike almost every other shark, they do not stop at the coast.

## 3. Salt

**Visual:** Animation: a shark cutaway with salt dots inside and outside; in the sea, balanced; in a river, water rushing in through the skin and gills, the body swelling. Real footage: a river meeting the sea, brown water into blue.

To understand why this is so strange, you need to know one thing about sharks.

A shark's body is full of salt and other chemicals. In the sea, that matches the water around it. Nothing leaks in or out.

But in fresh water, the outside has almost no salt. Water floods into the shark's body through its gills and skin, trying to even things out. Its blood thins. Its cells swell.

For most sharks, a river is a slow poison. That is why you almost never see them there.

The bull shark has solved that problem.

## 4. How it survives

**Visual:** Animation: the kidneys working overtime, a stream of dilute urine; the rectal gland shrinking; salt held in. Real photo: a bull shark in shallow, murky water.

When a bull shark swims from the sea into a river, its body changes.

Its kidneys go into overdrive. They pump out huge amounts of very weak, watery urine, getting rid of the extra water as fast as it comes in.

At the same time, its kidneys hold on to salt, pulling it back into the blood instead of losing it.

And a special gland that normally dumps extra salt, the rectal gland, slows down. In fresh water, there is no extra salt to dump.

Its blood ends up less salty than it is in the sea. But never as watery as the river. The shark keeps itself somewhere in between, balanced on a knife edge.

It is not effortless. It costs the shark energy. But it works. And it opens up a whole world that other sharks cannot reach.

## 5. Why go upriver at all?

**Visual:** Animation: a pregnant female heading into an estuary; pups in a shallow brown lagoon; a big tiger shark turning back at the river mouth. Real photo: a young bull shark (iNaturalist).

So why do it?

Many bull sharks are born in rivers and estuaries. Pregnant females swim into brackish water to give birth, and the young stay there for years.

It is a nursery. The water is murky, full of fish, and most of the big sharks that might eat a young bull shark will not follow it inside.

Scientists who tag young bull sharks in rivers often find them staying in the same few kilometres of river for months, moving up and down with the tide and the salt, hiding in the brown water.

As they grow, the young move further out to sea. But the river stays part of their world for life.

And the water they grow up in is exactly the kind of water where people wade, wash, fish and swim.

## 6. It is already following you

**Visual:** Back to the river. Animation: you in the water; the shark's view: no picture, just a cloud of signals around your legs. Close-ups of the shark's head: the lateral line, the pores of the electrosense, the nostrils.

Back in the river.

You cannot see more than a hand's length into the water. Neither can the shark.

But it does not need to.

Along each side of its body runs a line of sensitive pores, the lateral line. It feels movement in the water. The splash of your feet. The kick of your legs.

Its nostrils can pick up blood and body fluids in tiny amounts.

And in the skin of its snout are hundreds of jelly-filled pores. They sense the faint electric fields given off by every living muscle. Including yours.

Those pores are among the most sensitive electric sensors in the animal world. They can pick up fields of just a few billionths of a volt across a centimetre of water. Every beat of your heart, every twitch of your legs, sends out a tiny signal. And in the dark brown water, the shark is reading it.

## 7. In water you cannot see through

**Visual:** Animation: murky water; a fish shape and a human foot side by side, both glowing as the same kind of signal. Card: MURKY WATER · DUSK · RIVER MOUTHS. Real footage: brown floodwater.

This is the danger of murky water.

To a bull shark hunting in a brown river, a splashing foot and a struggling fish can feel very similar.

It cannot take a long look first. So sometimes, it simply tests the shape with a bump, or a bite.

That is why most bites from bull sharks happen in places just like this. Murky water. River mouths. Harbours. Often after heavy rain, when rivers pour brown water and dead fish into the sea, and the sharks come in to feed.

## 8. The bump

**Visual:** Animation: the shark circling, then a head-butt against your leg; you stagger. Then the turn. Card: THE "BUMP AND BITE".

So the first thing you may feel is not a bite.

It is a bump. A hard knock against your leg or your side, from that blunt snout.

Bull sharks often bump what they are curious about. It may be how they feel out whether something is food, and whether it fights back.

If you are lucky, it swims away.

But sometimes, the bump comes first and the bite comes next.

## 9. The bite

**Visual:** Animation: the jaw from the side; upper teeth broad and serrated, lower teeth narrow and pointed, like a fork and a knife. A bar chart: bite force for its size. Real photo: bull shark jaws (Openverse).

A bull shark's jaws are built for big, struggling prey.

The lower teeth are narrow and pointed, to hold. The upper teeth are wide, triangular and serrated, to cut. A fork and a knife.

In 2012, scientists compared the bite force of thirteen kinds of sharks and rays. For its body size, the bull shark had the strongest bite of them all. Stronger, pound for pound, than a great white.

For a big bull shark, the team calculated a bite at the back of the jaw of nearly six thousand newtons. Like the weight of about six hundred kilograms, pressing down on a few square centimetres.

Bull sharks are one of the three sharks behind most serious unprovoked bites on people, along with the great white and the tiger shark.

## 10. Sydney Harbour, 2009

**Visual:** Map: Sydney Harbour. Real photo: Sydney Harbour, Woolloomooloo Bay (Openverse). Animation: a diver on the surface at dawn; a shape below. Then: a swimmer recovering, a prosthetic leg (drawn).

In February 2009, a Navy diver called Paul de Gelder was in the water in Sydney Harbour, taking part in a training exercise at dawn.

A bull shark, more than two and a half metres long, came up from below and bit him. It took his right hand and his right leg.

His team pulled him from the water. He survived, and went on to become a campaigner for protecting sharks.

The harbour he was in was not a remote river. It was the middle of one of the biggest cities in Australia. Bull sharks still swim there every summer.

## 11. Where they turn up

**Visual:** Map montage with pins: Lake Nicaragua and the Rio San Juan; Alton, Illinois on the Mississippi; the Brisbane River; Carbrook golf course. Real photos of each place (Openverse). Card: 1937 · ALTON.

And bull sharks have turned up in some very unlikely places.

In Central America, sharks were found in Lake Nicaragua, a huge freshwater lake. For years, people thought they were a separate, trapped species. In the late 1960s, scientists tagged more than two thousand of them, and proved they were bull sharks swimming up and down the San Juan River, through its rapids, between the lake and the sea.

In 1937, two fishermen pulled a bull shark out of the Mississippi River at Alton, Illinois. More than a thousand kilometres from the sea.

And in Australia, after a flood in the 1990s, six bull sharks ended up in a lake on a golf course near Brisbane. They lived there for well over a decade, watched by golfers, until they disappeared.

Floods like that are part of the bull shark's world. Every time a big hurricane or flood hits, photos of sharks swimming down flooded streets go viral. Almost all of them are fakes. But the golf course was real. When rivers burst their banks, bull sharks can go where the water goes.

## 12. The farthest from the sea

**Visual:** Map: the Amazon from its mouth to Iquitos, a line drawn upriver for 4,000 km. Real photo: the Amazon at Iquitos (Openverse). Animation: a bull shark small against the huge river.

But the record is in South America.

Bull sharks have been found far up the Amazon, at Iquitos in Peru.

That is around four thousand kilometres from the Atlantic Ocean. Further than the distance from London to Cairo, in fresh water, against the current, all the way.

Iquitos sits more than a hundred metres above sea level. To get there, the shark had to swim uphill. For weeks. Through one of the biggest rivers on the planet.

A sea shark, swimming in the heart of the rainforest.

And the Amazon is not the only great river it climbs. In southern Africa, bull sharks swim up the Zambezi. In Australia, they live far up the rivers of Queensland and the Northern Territory. Anywhere a warm river meets a warm sea, the bull shark may be waiting.

## 13. If it follows you

**Visual:** Animation: a checklist on screen as the narrator speaks; you moving slowly back towards the bank; facing the shark. Real footage: a river bank.

So, what should you do if you are ever in water where bull sharks live?

Most of it is about not being there at the wrong time. Avoid swimming in murky water, in river mouths, and in harbours, especially at dawn, at dusk, and after heavy rain. Do not swim near people fishing, or near anything bleeding. Take off shiny jewellery, which can flash like the scales of a fish. And stay with other people. A shark is more likely to approach someone alone.

If you see a shark, do not splash. Keep it in sight, and move calmly towards the shore or the boat.

And if it does bite, fight back. Hit the eyes and the gills, the most sensitive parts. Most sharks that bite people let go.

## 14. The other side

**Visual:** Real footage: a fishing market; a bull shark on a line. Animation: a river dammed and polluted; a counter of nursery rivers lost. Card: IUCN · VULNERABLE.

But the danger mostly runs the other way.

For every person bitten by a bull shark, huge numbers of bull sharks are caught by people. For their meat, their fins, their skin and their liver oil.

The rivers and estuaries where bull sharks are born are some of the most damaged waters on Earth. Dams block them. Pollution and building fill them. Nets and lines catch the young.

Bull sharks are listed as vulnerable, and in many places their numbers have fallen.

The odds of a bull shark biting you are tiny. The odds of people harming a bull shark are not.

## 15. Close

**Visual:** Back to the brown river at dusk. The shape turns away from your legs and slides downstream towards the distant sea. End card.

So what happens when a bull shark follows you upriver?

Most of the time, nothing. It feels your legs move, decides you are not a fish, and turns away into the brown water.

You never even know it was there.

Because that is the strangest thing about the bull shark. It is not hunting you. Most of the time, it is just living its life in a place we never expected a shark to be.

But it was. A sea shark, hundreds of kilometres from the sea, in water that should have killed it.

And the next time you stand in a warm, brown river, you will wonder what is moving just past your feet.
