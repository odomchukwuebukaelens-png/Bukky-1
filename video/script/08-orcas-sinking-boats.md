# The Orcas That Started Sinking Boats

Video 2 of the 60-title list. Format: true story (chronological, told as one unfolding case). English, "Deep sea" voice. Target 11 minutes (about 2,000 words).
Music: the crocodile tracks (owner, 26 Nov 2026: videos 1 and 2 reuse the music we have).

Structure: a cold open on the Champagne (May 2023), then the story from the beginning: the strait, the tuna hunters, the first rudder in 2020, the Gladises, each sinking in order, the theories, the scientists' answer, 2025, and the 2026 turn (Toñi shot). The opening is a story moment, not "you" in the water, and not a depth descent, so it is unlike videos 1 and 3.

## 1. Cold open

**Visual:** VFX: a white sailing yacht motoring at dusk in the Strait of Gibraltar. Underwater: three black-and-white shapes rising behind the stern. The rudder from below. A hard impact. Map insert: the Strait. Title. The three promises.

On the fourth of May, 2023, a Swiss sailing yacht called Champagne was motoring through the Strait of Gibraltar.

Then the crew felt the first hit.

Three orcas had come up behind the boat. The biggest one rammed the hull from the side. The two smaller ones went straight for the rudder, and shook it.

The crew noticed something strange. The two small orcas were copying the big one. Watching, then doing the same thing. Like they were being taught.

By the time it was over, the rudder had two holes punched through it. The boat was taking on water. And the orcas were gone.

This was not an accident. It was not the first time. And it would not be the last.

So why did one small family of orcas start breaking boats?

In this story, you will find out who started it. Why they only go for the rudder. And what happens when people start fighting back.

## 2. The narrow door

**Visual:** Map: Europe and Africa almost touching at the Strait of Gibraltar; ships streaming through. Real footage: a cargo ship and a sailboat on a bright sea. Photo: the Rock of Gibraltar.

To understand this story, you have to start with a place.

The Strait of Gibraltar is a narrow door between two continents. Spain on one side. Morocco on the other. At its tightest, only about fourteen kilometres of water separate Europe from Africa.

Every ship that wants to go between the Atlantic and the Mediterranean has to squeeze through it. Tankers. Cargo ships. Ferries. Fishing boats.

And every spring, thousands of sailing yachts, heading south for the sun.

But the strait is also a door for something else.

## 3. The tuna hunters

**Visual:** Photo (iNaturalist): Atlantic bluefin tuna. Animation: a tuna bolting, an orca on its tail, a long chase; the tuna slowing. Real footage: orcas surfacing. Photo: a fishing boat and a drop line.

Every spring, huge Atlantic bluefin tuna pour through the strait on their way to the Mediterranean.

And waiting for them is a small group of orcas.

They are not like the orcas you might know from the cold north. These are tuna specialists. They chase a single tuna for a long time, matching it turn for turn, until the fish is too tired to escape.

Some of them learned a shortcut, too. When local fishermen haul up tuna on long lines, a few of these orcas simply wait beside the boat and take the fish off the hook.

They follow the tuna. Through the strait in spring and summer. Then north, along the coast of Portugal, up to Galicia in the far north of Spain.

## 4. Thirty-nine

**Visual:** Animation: 39 orca icons in five little groups. Number counter. Red List stamp: CRITICALLY ENDANGERED. Photo (iNaturalist): an orca mother and calf in the strait.

These orcas are called the Iberian orcas, and there are very few of them.

When scientists counted every single one in 2011, they found thirty-nine. Thirty-nine animals, in five family groups.

That's it. That is the whole population.

In 2019, the International Union for Conservation of Nature listed them as critically endangered. One bad decade could wipe them out.

For years, almost nobody outside Spain and Portugal knew they existed.

That was about to change.

## 5. The first rudder

**Visual:** Calendar: May 2020. Animation: an empty sea, one sailboat; a fin; the wheel turning by itself in the cockpit. Map pins: the Strait, then Portugal, then Galicia.

In May 2020, the world had gone quiet. Ports were closed. Most boats stayed tied up.

That month, in the Strait of Gibraltar, an orca came up behind a sailboat and touched the rudder.

Then it happened again in July. In the strait, and off the coast of Portugal. By August, sailors far to the north, off Galicia, were reporting the same thing.

Orcas, coming up from behind. Pushing the rudder. And at the wheel, the sailors felt it turn in their hands, all on its own.

Between July and November of 2020, fifty-two of these encounters were reported.

Scientists from Spain and Portugal set up a working group in August that year, just to keep track of it.

## 6. How they do it

**Visual:** Animation (cutaway): a yacht from below, the keel and the spade rudder. An orca pushes the rudder blade with its head; the boat pivots almost a full circle. Close-up: tooth marks on fibreglass. A torn-off rudder.

What they do is strangely precise.

The orcas come in quietly from behind. They ignore the hull, the sails, the people. They go for one part of the boat: the rudder.

They push it with their heads. They lever it with their bodies. Some of them bite it. With enough pushing, they can spin a boat almost all the way around.

When the boats were checked afterwards, people found tooth marks raked across the keel and the rudder. Some rudders were bent. Some were split in half. Some were simply gone. At least one orca was seen tearing a rudder off with its teeth.

And they chose their targets. Most were sailing boats about twelve metres long, moving at around six knots. Slow enough for an orca to catch with ease.

## 7. The Gladises

**Visual:** Photo-ID cards (drawn fins with name tags, in the style of a research catalogue): Gladis Blanca, Gladis Filabres, Gladis Dalila, Gladis Clara; Gladis Gris, Gladis Peque, Gladis Negra. Real photo (iNaturalist): a dorsal fin close-up.

Scientists can tell every orca apart by the shape of its fin and the grey patch behind it. So they started to work out exactly who was doing this.

They gave every orca that touched a boat the same first name: Gladis. It comes from an old scientific name for the orca, gladiator.

In a study published in 2022, they found that nine orcas had touched the boats. They came from two families.

In the first family was an adult female called Gladis Blanca. White Gladis. With her were her sisters, Gladis Dalila and Gladis Clara, and later her own calf, born in 2021.

The second family was a gang of youngsters. Gladis Gris, Grey Gladis. And two siblings, Gladis Peque and Gladis Negra. Black Gladis.

By 2023, the list had grown to fifteen.

## 8. The first boats go down

**Visual:** Map: Portugal's coast, two pins. Animation: a yacht settling by the stern, a life raft, a rescue helicopter. Number: 197, then 207.

For two years, the orcas broke rudders and left. Boats were towed into port, repaired and sent back out.

The encounters kept coming. One hundred and ninety-seven in 2021. Two hundred and seven in 2022.

Then, in July 2022, off the coast of Portugal, a sailboat with five people aboard took so much damage that it sank.

In November, it happened again. A yacht called Smousse, with four people aboard, went down off Portugal.

Everyone was rescued both times. But now the story had changed.

## 9. Champagne

**Visual:** Return to the cold open. Animation: the big orca rams, the two small orcas copy it at the rudder. Coast guard boat, tow line. Photo: Barbate harbour. The yacht rolls over at the harbour mouth.

And that brings us back to the Champagne, in May 2023.

When the three orcas had finished with her, the rudder was pierced and its fittings were broken. Spain's coast guard took the crew off, and towed the yacht towards the port of Barbate.

She made it all the way to the harbour entrance. And there, she rolled over.

What frightened people most was what the crew had seen. One big orca, showing the way. Two smaller ones, copying it.

It looked like a lesson.

## 10. The revenge story

**Visual:** Animation: newspaper headlines and phone screens filling the frame: ORCA UPRISING, ORCA WARS. A drawn fishing net, a propeller, White Gladis alone. MYTH-style question mark, not a stamp.

Within days, the orcas were everywhere online.

People made memes about an orca uprising. Orca wars. Orcas fighting back against humans.

And a story spread about White Gladis. The idea was that, years earlier, she had been hurt. Maybe hit by a boat. Maybe caught in an illegal fishing net. And now she was taking revenge, and teaching the young ones to do the same.

It is a powerful story. Scientists did think about it. A painful run-in with a boat could have started the behaviour.

But no one has ever found proof that it happened.

## 11. More boats

**Visual:** Map: three pins in the strait. Animation: a yacht sinking near a port's breakwater; a tanker turning to pick up two people; a coast guard boat with three survivors.

The orcas did not stop.

On the thirty-first of October 2023, a yacht called Grazie Mamma Two met a group of them in the strait. For forty-five minutes they bumped and pushed the rudder, until the boat was leaking. It sank near the entrance to the port of Tanger Med, in Morocco.

On the twelfth of May 2024, a Spanish yacht called Alboran Cognac was holed in the strait. The two people aboard were picked up by a passing tanker. The yacht sank.

Ten weeks later, a British yacht called Bonhomie William went down in the same waters. Spain's coast guard rescued all three people aboard.

## 12. Why the rudder?

**Visual:** Split screen: an orca hitting a tuna's tail; an orca hitting a rudder. Animation: the propeller spinning, an orca pressing against it. A kid's toy-like spin of the yacht.

So why the rudder, again and again?

One idea is that it is the boat's weak spot. Orcas hunt by going for the tail of their prey, because the tail is what lets it swim away. The rudder sits exactly where a fish's tail would be.

Another researcher noticed that the orcas seem to love the feeling of a spinning propeller. And when a sailboat turns its engine off, maybe they go after the rudder out of frustration.

And then there is the simplest idea of all. A rudder is the one part of a boat that moves when you push it. Push it, and the whole boat turns.

For a young orca, that might be the best toy in the ocean.

## 13. What the scientists decided

**Visual:** Photo: a conference room (Madrid). Animation: a well-fed orca beside a tuna shoal; a clock; orcas playing. Insert: the salmon-hat orca, Puget Sound, 1987 (drawn).

In February 2024, scientists, sailors and government officials met in Madrid to look at all of it together.

They found no evidence that the orcas were being aggressive.

Here is what they think is going on. In recent years, bluefin tuna numbers in this region have come back. For the first time in generations, these orcas have plenty to eat. And when you are well fed, you have time on your hands.

Orcas have done this kind of thing before. In 1987, off the coast of Washington State, one orca started swimming around with a dead salmon balanced on its head. Within weeks, others in her group were doing it too. Then, the next year, they all stopped.

Scientists call it a fad. The rudders may be the same thing.

## 14. Stop the boat

**Visual:** Animation: a yacht with the engine cut and the sails down, the crew still; the orcas circling, then leaving. A firework splashes; the orcas come back harder. Text: STOP · LET GO OF THE WHEEL · REPORT IT.

That also explains what works.

When crews fought the wheel or sped up, the orcas pushed harder and more often. When the crews stopped the boat and let go, the orcas usually got bored and left.

Some sailors tried something else. Firecrackers dropped into the water. Even electric shocks.

The scientists' verdict was clear. These things do not stop the orcas. They may even make the game more exciting.

And here is the part that matters most. In all these years, in more than five hundred encounters, no one aboard has ever been hurt by these orcas.

If they wanted to sink boats, scientists point out, they could have sunk hundreds by now.

## 15. When people fight back

**Visual:** Map: Lisbon, Fonte da Telha, Cascais, Peniche. Animation: a small tourist yacht settling bow-first; a family in a life raft and a fishing boat arriving. Then: the strait, summer 2026, a large older orca surfacing; close-up of a drawn dorsal fin with about ten small holes. Photo: the strait at sunset.

In 2025, the game moved north.

On the thirteenth of September, a tourist yacht with five people aboard was struck again and again near Fonte da Telha, just south of Lisbon. It went down bow first. All five were rescued. That same day, another boat was hit further up the coast, off Cascais.

Four weeks later, a French yacht called Ti'fare sank about fifty nautical miles off Peniche. The family of five aboard climbed into a life raft and were picked up by a fishing boat.

Then, in August 2026, people in the strait spotted Toñi. She is the oldest orca in the Strait of Gibraltar.

On the left side of her dorsal fin, there were about ten small holes. Spain's environment ministry said they were consistent with pellet shots, and opened an investigation.

Just weeks earlier, her fin had been unmarked.

## 16. An old story

**Visual:** Archive-style animation: 1972, a wooden schooner in the Pacific, orcas around it, the hull holed, a family in a tiny dinghy. Calendar: 38 days. Back to the strait today.

This is not the first time orcas have sunk a boat.

In June 1972, west of the Galapagos Islands, a family called the Robertsons was sailing around the world on a wooden schooner called Lucette.

A group of killer whales struck the hull. Within a minute, the boat was gone.

The family survived thirty-eight days adrift in a tiny dinghy, before a Japanese fishing boat found them.

What makes the Iberian orcas different is not that they hit a boat once. It is that a whole family learned it. And kept doing it, year after year.

## 17. Close

**Visual:** Animation: the 39 icons again; a few fade. A yacht passes through the strait at sunset, orcas surfacing far behind it, not following. End card.

So who are the real victims in this story?

The orcas have broken hundreds of rudders and sunk a handful of boats. Every single person aboard those boats has gone home.

But there are still fewer than forty Iberian orcas. Every one of them has a name. And now one of the oldest has holes in her fin.

Fads end. Young orcas grow up. One day, the Gladises may simply stop, the way the salmon hats stopped.

The question is whether people will give them the chance.
