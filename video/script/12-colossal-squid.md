## 1. Cold open

**Visual:** VFX: an ROV's lights in black water, depth 600 m. A small glassy shape drifts into the beam. Animation: the same small squid, then a ghost outline of a 500-kilogram adult growing behind it. Title. The three promises.

On the ninth of March, 2025, a robot was exploring the dark sea off the South Sandwich Islands, near Antarctica.

About six hundred metres down, its lights picked out something small and glassy. A young squid, about thirty centimetres long, almost completely see-through.

The scientists watching on the ship did not know it yet. The pictures were being streamed live on the internet, and almost nobody watching knew what they were seeing either. But they had just made history.

It was a colossal squid. The heaviest invertebrate on Earth. An animal that can grow to around five hundred kilograms.

And it was the first time anyone had ever seen one alive, in its home.

It took a hundred years to get here. So what kind of animal hides from us for a century?

In this video, you will see the biggest eye of any animal, the hooks that swivel on its arms, and why its brain has a hole through the middle.

## 2. A hundred years of pieces

**Visual:** Archive-style animation: 1925, a sperm whale's stomach opened on a whaling ship, two arm pieces and beaks. Card: 1925 · MESONYCHOTEUTHIS HAMILTONI. Real photo: a sperm whale (iNaturalist).

The colossal squid was first named in 1925.

Not from a living animal. From two pieces of arm, found inside the stomach of a sperm whale.

For decades after that, almost everything we knew about it came from inside whales. Hard, parrot-like beaks, the one part of a squid that does not digest, piled up in sperm whale stomachs by the thousand.

From those beaks, scientists could tell that something very big was living in the Southern Ocean. And that sperm whales were eating a lot of it.

Colossal squid beaks are the biggest beaks of any squid ever found.

But nobody had seen one whole.

## 3. The fishing boat

**Visual:** Map: New Zealand to the Ross Sea. Animation: a longline boat at night; a toothfish on the line; a huge red mantle rising beside the hull. Real photo: a Patagonian or Antarctic toothfish (iNaturalist). Card: FEBRUARY 2007 · 495 KG.

Then, in February 2007, a New Zealand fishing boat called the San Aspiring was hauling longlines in the Ross Sea, off Antarctica.

The crew were catching toothfish, big deep-sea fish. And one of them was being eaten as it came up. By a colossal squid that would not let go.

The squid was too big to lift with nets alone. The crew wrapped it, hauled it aboard, and froze it.

Back in New Zealand, at the museum Te Papa, scientists thawed it and weighed it.

It stayed frozen for more than a year. When it was finally thawed, in 2008, the museum streamed the whole thing online, and people around the world watched a colossal squid slowly soften in a tank.

Four hundred and ninety-five kilograms. About as heavy as a grand piano. The largest invertebrate ever recorded.

## 4. Colossal, not giant

**Visual:** Animation: the giant squid and the colossal squid side by side. The giant: longer, thinner, very long tentacles. The colossal: shorter, much heavier mantle, hooks. Number cards: GIANT ≈ 12 M LONG; COLOSSAL ≈ 500 KG.

You may have heard of the giant squid. The colossal squid is a different animal.

The giant squid is longer. With its two feeding tentacles stretched out, it can reach around twelve metres. But much of that length is thin tentacle.

The colossal squid is shorter, but far bulkier. Its body, the mantle, is thick and heavy. It weighs much more than any giant squid ever measured.

The giant squid lives in oceans all over the world. The colossal squid lives only around Antarctica, in some of the coldest water on the planet.

And it has a few weapons the giant squid does not. The giant squid's suckers are ringed with small teeth. The colossal squid has those too. But it also has hooks.

## 5. The rest of the body

**Visual:** Animation: the colossal squid labelled part by part: mantle, fins, eight arms, two tentacles, beak, eye. Real photo: the Te Papa specimen if free; otherwise a museum squid specimen (Openverse, labelled).

Let us go through it, piece by piece.

At the back is the mantle, a big muscular tube. In the 2007 animal, the mantle alone was about two and a half metres long. Inside it are the squid's organs. When it squeezes the mantle, water shoots out of a funnel, and the squid jets backwards.

On the mantle are two broad fins, for slow, gentle swimming.

At the front are eight arms, and two longer tentacles, for grabbing prey.

In the middle of all those arms is the mouth. And beside the head, two eyes.

Each part of this animal is built for one thing. Hunting in near-freezing darkness, deep below the Southern Ocean.

## 6. The biggest eye in the world

**Visual:** Animation: the eye beside a dinner plate, a football and a human eye. Number card: ≈ 27 CM · then ≈ 35 CM (2014 specimen). Animation: a sperm whale's glow trail in the dark, seen from far away.

Start with the eye.

The colossal squid caught in 2007 had eyes about twenty-seven centimetres across. Bigger than a dinner plate.

In 2014, another colossal squid was examined in New Zealand. Its eyes were measured at around thirty-five centimetres.

These are the biggest eyes known in the animal kingdom.

Why would an animal need eyes that big, where there is almost no light?

One idea is that giant eyes are not for spotting small prey. They are for spotting something huge, from far away. When a sperm whale dives through the dark, it disturbs tiny glowing creatures in the water, and leaves a faint trail of light. A squid with giant eyes may see that trail coming, and have a little more time to escape.

In 2012, a team of scientists calculated what eyes this big could do. In the deep sea, they could pick out the glow around a large animal like a sperm whale from more than a hundred metres away. Smaller eyes would see it much later. Too late.

## 7. The swivelling hooks

**Visual:** Animation: close-up of an arm: suckers with hooks; the tentacle club with hooks that rotate. Real photo: hook scars on sperm whale skin (Openverse, if free). Card: HOOKS THAT SWIVEL.

Next, the arms.

Most squid have suckers, rimmed with tiny teeth. The colossal squid has something extra.

Its eight arms carry suckers and sharp hooks. And at the ends of its two long tentacles are hooks that can swivel.

When the tentacles grab a fish, the hooks turn and dig in. The harder the prey struggles, the deeper they go.

Some sperm whales carry round scars and long scratches on their skin, from squid fighting back in the dark.

The colossal squid's hooks are part of how we know it does not go quietly.

## 8. The beak

**Visual:** Animation: the beak opening and closing; a cutaway of the head with the beak inside the ring of arms. Real photo: squid beaks (Openverse / iNaturalist). Card: THE ONLY HARD PART.

Inside the ring of arms is the beak.

It looks like a parrot's beak. It is hard and sharp, and it is the only truly hard part of the squid's body.

The beak cuts prey into small pieces. Then a tongue covered in tiny teeth, called a radula, pulls those pieces in.

And there is a very good reason the pieces have to be small.

## 9. The brain with a hole in it

**Visual:** Animation: a cutaway of the head: a doughnut-shaped brain, the oesophagus running straight through the hole. A piece of food that is too big pressing on the ring. Card: THE FOOD PASSES THROUGH THE BRAIN.

The colossal squid's brain is shaped like a doughnut.

And running straight through the hole in the middle is its oesophagus, the tube that carries food to the stomach.

Every bite it swallows passes through the middle of its brain.

This is true for squid and their relatives in general. And it means that if a squid swallowed a piece of food that was too big, it could press on its own brain.

Octopuses and cuttlefish share the same strange design. It is one of the oddest facts in the animal world.

So the beak and the radula do careful work. Small pieces only. Even for an animal that weighs half a tonne.

## 10. Three hearts and blue blood

**Visual:** Animation: three hearts pumping, one for the body and one for each gill; blood shown blue. Card: 3 HEARTS · BLUE BLOOD.

Inside the mantle, there is more strangeness.

Like other squid, the colossal squid has three hearts. Two of them pump blood through the gills, to pick up oxygen. The third pumps it around the rest of the body.

And its blood is blue.

Our blood uses iron to carry oxygen, which makes it red. Squid blood uses copper, which turns it blue.

In very cold water, with little oxygen, it is a system that works.

## 11. The slowest giant

**Visual:** Animation: the squid hanging still in the dark, barely moving; a single toothfish drifting close; the tentacles striking. A counter: 5 KG FISH = ≈ 200 DAYS.

For a long time, people imagined the colossal squid as a fast, aggressive monster of the deep.

In 2010, two scientists looked at how much energy an animal like this would need in water close to freezing.

Their answer surprised everyone. The colossal squid probably lives very slowly.

By their estimate, a single five-kilogram toothfish could keep a five-hundred-kilogram colossal squid going for around two hundred days.

So instead of chasing prey, it may hang almost still in the dark, waiting. Watching with those huge eyes. And when a fish comes close enough, the hooked tentacles strike.

Not a hunter that chases. A hunter that waits.

In water this cold, close to freezing, a slow life makes sense. Every movement costs energy, and food is hard to find. The best plan is to waste nothing.

## 12. Glass

**Visual:** Real photos: glass squid (Cranchiidae) from iNaturalist, see-through with a few dots of colour. Animation: a tiny transparent squid growing, its body slowly filling in with colour and thickness as it grows and sinks deeper.

The colossal squid belongs to a family called glass squids.

When they are young, many glass squids are almost completely transparent. You can see right through them, to the few organs inside.

In the open ocean, being see-through is one of the best ways to hide.

Many glass squids also carry a special fluid inside their bodies, rich in ammonia. It is lighter than sea water, so it helps them float without effort. Like a built-in life jacket.

As they grow, glass squids move deeper. And their bodies change. The colossal squid grows from a small, clear, delicate animal into a red, heavy, five-hundred-kilogram giant.

That is why the one filmed in 2025 looked so unlike the adults people knew.

## 13. Its only real enemy

**Visual:** Real photo: a sperm whale (iNaturalist). Animation: a sperm whale diving, clicks spreading out as rings; the squid's eye catching the glow trail; the chase in darkness. Card: BEAKS IN SPERM WHALE STOMACHS.

A full-grown colossal squid has very few enemies.

But one hunts it on purpose. The sperm whale.

Sperm whales dive deep into the Southern Ocean, finding prey with sound, clicks so loud they bounce off anything in the dark. They can dive more than a kilometre down and stay there for over an hour, searching.

When scientists counted the squid beaks inside sperm whales caught in parts of the Southern Ocean, colossal squid beaks were only a small share of the total. But because each one came from such a heavy animal, colossal squid may have made up a large part of what those whales ate.

Every colossal squid beak we have ever found tells the story of a fight we have never seen.

## 14. A century to see it

**Visual:** Back to the ROV footage animation: the small glassy squid drifting in the lights. A timeline: 1925 → 2007 → 2014 → 2025. Card: 100 YEARS.

So what did the 2025 footage show?

A young colossal squid, around thirty centimetres long, drifting in the dark. Its eight arms folded around its head. Glass-clear, with tiny specks of colour.

The scientists only confirmed what it was after experts studied the video. The hooks on its arms gave it away.

It had taken exactly a hundred years, from the first pieces in a whale's stomach in 1925, to the first time we saw one alive.

And it was not the expedition's only first. A few weeks earlier, the same ship had filmed another Antarctic squid alive for the first time, the glacial glass squid. The deep Southern Ocean is still full of animals we have barely seen.

## 15. Close

**Visual:** Animation: the young squid sinking out of the lights into darkness; far below, the ghost of a 500 kg adult. Real footage: the deep sea. End card.

Nobody has ever seen an adult colossal squid alive in its home.

Somewhere below the Southern Ocean, the big ones are still out there. Hanging in the cold. Watching with the biggest eyes on Earth. Waiting.

Maybe one day a camera will find one.

Until then, all we have is one small, glassy young squid, drifting through a robot's lights for a few minutes. And a hundred years of wondering.
