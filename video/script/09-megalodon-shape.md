## 1. Cold open

**Visual:** Archive photo: the 1909 jaw reconstruction at the American Museum of Natural History with people seated inside it. Animation: a giant great white silhouette swelling across the screen, then cracking like glass. Title. The three promises.

In 1909, a museum in New York put a shark's jaw on display.

It was so big that people could sit inside it, with room to spare.

It was built around real fossil teeth. And from the size of that jaw, people imagined a shark that could have been thirty metres long.

There was just one problem. The jaw was wrong. And so was almost everything people pictured behind it.

For more than a century, we have imagined megalodon as a great white shark, only bigger. The same face. The same thick body. Just scaled up until it could swallow a car.

But in the last few years, scientists have started to say something surprising. Megalodon probably did not look like that at all.

So what did the biggest shark that ever lived really look like?

On the way, you will find out why every picture of megalodon is a guess. What one spine in a museum revealed. And the shape scientists now think it had.

## 2. Tongue stones

**Visual:** Real photos: a fossil megalodon tooth in a hand. Archive: Steno's 1667 engraving of a shark head with teeth. Animation: a Renaissance cabinet with "tongue stones" in drawers. Insert: Nicolas Steno's portrait.

For centuries, people in Europe found strange stones in rocks and cliffs.

They were shaped like triangles. Some were as big as a hand. They had sharp, sawlike edges.

People called them tongue stones. Some believed they were the tongues of snakes, turned to stone. Others thought they fell from the sky.

In 1667, a Danish anatomist called Nicolas Steno published his study of a great white shark's head, sent to him in Florence.

He looked at its teeth, row after row of them. And he argued that the tongue stones were not tongues at all. They were teeth. The teeth of sharks that had lived long ago.

He was not the first to say it. But he made the case so clearly that it helped start a new science: the study of fossils.

Some of those tongue stones were megalodon teeth.

## 3. A shark made of teeth

**Visual:** Real photos: megalodon teeth beside a great white tooth, a hand for scale. Animation: a shark skeleton in grey cartilage dissolving, leaving only teeth and a few vertebrae. Card: MEGALODON = "BIG TOOTH".

And that is still almost all we have.

A shark's skeleton is not made of bone. It is made of cartilage, the same bendy material as the tip of your nose. When a shark dies, the cartilage rots away. Almost nothing is left.

Except the teeth.

Sharks shed teeth all their lives, thousands of them. So megalodon left behind teeth everywhere. On every continent except Antarctica.

The biggest ones are around eighteen centimetres long. Nearly three times the length of a great white's tooth.

In the 1800s, the Swiss naturalist Louis Agassiz gave the species its name. Megalodon. It simply means "big tooth".

But imagine trying to draw an animal you have never seen, when all you have is its teeth.

## 4. The great white shortcut

**Visual:** Animation: a megalodon tooth and a great white tooth side by side, both triangles with serrated edges. A great white silhouette scaled up, up, up. Real photo: a great white (iNaturalist).

So scientists took a shortcut.

Megalodon teeth are triangles with serrated edges. So are the teeth of the great white shark. They look like the same design, just bigger.

So for a long time, megalodon was treated as a kind of giant great white. It was even placed in the same genus, Carcharodon.

If the teeth were like a great white's, why not the rest of it?

Take a great white. Make its teeth three times bigger. Then make the whole shark three times bigger to match.

That is the megalodon you have seen in films, books and documentaries. A great white, scaled up.

And that is exactly what the museum in 1909 did.

## 5. The jaw that was too big

**Visual:** Archive: the 1909 jaw again, close on the people inside. Animation: the jaw shrinking to a later, smaller reconstruction. Number card: 30 M → much less.

The man behind the 1909 jaw was Bashford Dean, a fish expert at the museum.

He set real megalodon teeth into a jaw shaped like a great white's. But he made the cartilage of the jaw far too big, and filled it with teeth that were too large for their places.

So the jaw came out enormous. And so did the shark people imagined behind it. Around thirty metres long.

Later scientists rebuilt the jaw more carefully, and the estimates came down. By the late twentieth century, most of them put the biggest megalodons at around fifteen to eighteen metres.

Still gigantic. But here is the thing. All of those estimates still used the great white as the model.

## 6. The wrong family tree

**Visual:** Animation: a family tree. One branch: Otodus obliquus (55 million years ago) → megalodon. Another branch: a mako-like ancestor → great white. Real photos: a shortfin mako (iNaturalist), a great white.

And then the family tree fell apart.

When scientists looked closely at fossil teeth from millions of years of sharks, they found that megalodon's line goes back a long way. Its oldest clear ancestor was a shark called Otodus obliquus, which lived around fifty-five million years ago.

The great white's line is different. It seems to come from a smaller, mako-like shark.

So megalodon is not the great white's grandfather. They are distant cousins. Today, megalodon is placed in its own genus: Otodus.

Their teeth look alike because they did a similar job. Cutting big chunks out of big animals.

And that means there was never a good reason to give megalodon a great white's body.

This is why every picture of megalodon is a guess. Even the most famous ones are a great white in disguise.

## 7. The spine in Brussels

**Visual:** Real photo: fossil shark vertebrae (labelled). Animation: 141 discs lining up into a column, a CT scanner sweeping one disc, rings appearing like tree rings. Map insert: Belgium.

But there is one fossil that is different.

In a museum in Brussels, there is a set of megalodon vertebrae, the discs of a spine, all from one shark. One hundred and forty-one of them.

It is the most complete piece of a megalodon body ever found. And it has been studied again and again.

In 2021, scientists scanned three of the discs. Inside, like the rings of a tree, they counted forty-six growth bands. That shark was probably forty-six years old when it died, and around nine metres long.

Working backwards through the rings, they found something even stranger. When it was born, it was already about two metres long.

That is longer than most adult humans. Before birth, the babies likely grew by eating other eggs inside their mother, as some living sharks do.

## 8. The 2022 model

**Visual:** Animation: a 3D-style wireframe built from the column, teeth placed, great-white "flesh" wrapped over it. Number cards: 16 M, 61 TONNES. A killer whale silhouette fitting inside its stomach outline. An 8-metre whale and a two-month clock.

In 2022, a team used that spine to build the first 3D model of a megalodon's whole body.

They lined up the vertebrae. They added a scanned set of teeth. And then, to fill in the rest, they wrapped it in the shape of a great white shark.

Their megalodon was sixteen metres long and weighed more than sixty-one tonnes.

It was big enough to swallow prey the size of a killer whale. And after eating a single eight-metre whale, it might have been able to swim thousands of kilometres without eating again for about two months.

It was the best model yet. But it still had a great white's body.

## 9. Too long to be a great white

**Visual:** Animation: the vertebral column laid along a great white outline; the column runs past the tail. Then a slimmer outline. Real photo: a lemon shark (iNaturalist).

In 2024, another team looked at that same spine and found a problem.

Measured end to end, the column was about eleven metres long. That is just the spine. No head. No tail.

If megalodon had been shaped like a great white, that spine would belong to a shark of about nine metres. The numbers did not fit.

And the discs themselves were too thin. A great white has thick, heavy vertebrae, built to hold up a short, stocky body. Megalodon's were slimmer.

There was only one way to make it work. Megalodon's body must have been longer and slimmer than a great white's.

## 10. The real shape

**Visual:** Animation: the new megalodon draws itself: long, sleek body, smaller-looking head, long tail. Side-by-side with a great white and a school bus. Number card: UP TO ≈ 24 M.

In 2025, the same scientists went further. They compared megalodon's spine with the bodies of many living sharks, to find the shape that fits best.

The answer was not a great white. It was something closer to a lemon shark, or a giant, stretched version of a sandbar shark. Long and streamlined.

They also pointed out that a huge, tank-like great white body would be a terrible design at that size. It would cost too much energy just to push through the water. A long, slender body glides more easily.

With that shape, the biggest megalodons may have reached around twenty-four metres. As long as two buses.

Not a giant great white. Something leaner, longer and stranger. A giant of its own kind.

## 11. A warm-blooded giant

**Visual:** Animation: the slender megalodon with a warm orange glow along its core; cold blue water around it. Real photo: a fossil tooth. Number card: ≈ 27 °C.

The teeth had one more secret.

In 2023, scientists measured special forms of carbon and oxygen locked inside megalodon's tooth enamel. These can show the temperature of the body the tooth grew in.

Megalodon's body averaged about twenty-seven degrees Celsius. Warmer than the sea around it, and warmer than other sharks of its time.

Like great whites and makos today, it was probably partly warm-blooded. That makes a shark faster and stronger in cold water.

But it also comes at a price. A warm body needs a lot of food.

## 12. What it ate

**Visual:** Animation: the slender megalodon chasing a small baleen whale; a bite at the tail and flippers. Real photo: whale fossil bones (labelled). Map: the Miocene oceans.

And megalodon ate a lot.

Fossil whale bones from that time carry deep cuts and gouges that match megalodon teeth. Some are on the tail and the flippers, as if the shark attacked the parts that let a whale swim away.

Its world was full of whales, seals, sea cows and big fish. For millions of years, it was the largest predator in the sea.

## 13. Why it disappeared

**Visual:** Animation: a globe cooling, ice spreading at the poles, warm coastal seas shrinking. A great white appears beside the megalodon, both reaching for the same whale. Card: ≈ 3.6 MILLION YEARS AGO.

Then, around three and a half million years ago, megalodon vanished.

The planet was cooling. Warm, shallow coastal seas, where young megalodons grew up, were shrinking. Many of the whales it hunted were changing, or moving to cold waters a warm-bodied giant could not easily follow.

And there was a new rival.

In 2022, scientists compared the zinc in fossil teeth, which shows how high up the food chain an animal fed. Megalodon and early great whites overlapped. They may have been competing for the same food.

A smaller, more flexible hunter, against a huge one that needed enormous meals.

The great white is still here.

## 14. Still a guess

**Visual:** Animation: three megalodon outlines side by side: the 1909 giant, the 2022 great-white model, the 2025 slender shark. A question mark over each. Real photo: a tooth in a museum drawer.

So is this the final answer?

No. Not every scientist agrees. Some say the case for a slender body is still tentative. We have no head, no fins, no tail. One spine and a lot of teeth.

Until someone finds more of a megalodon, every picture of it, including the one in this video, is a best guess.

But the guesses are getting better. And they are moving away from the old idea.

## 15. Close

**Visual:** Animation: the slender megalodon passes the camera in blue water, long and quiet; the old great-white silhouette fades behind it. End card.

For more than a hundred years, we drew megalodon by copying the shark we knew.

But the biggest shark that ever lived was not a great white with the volume turned up.

It was longer. Leaner. Warm-blooded. Born bigger than a grown human. And it hunted whales for millions of years before the sea changed around it.

We still have not seen its face. All we have is its teeth.

And the teeth have been telling us, all along, that it was something we had never imagined.
