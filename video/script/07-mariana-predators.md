# Every Predator Waiting Between the Surface and the Mariana Trench

Video 1 of the 60-title list. Format: depth journey (not an encounter). English, "Deep sea" voice. Target 11 minutes (about 2,000 words).
Thumbnail text: 11 KM DOWN..
Music: the crocodile tracks (owner, 26 Nov 2026: videos 1 and 2 reuse the music we have).

Structure: one continuous descent above the Mariana Trench, a depth counter on the descent shots (centre-bottom, above the captions, not in a corner), each zone introduces a stranger hunter. The story turns at about 8,300 m, where fish stop. A true human story every two to three minutes (Indianapolis, Kubodera, Trieste, the plastic bag).
On-screen promises (scene 1), answered in scenes 5, 11 and 13:
1. The shark that bit nuclear submarines
2. The last fish before life gives up
3. What was waiting at the very bottom

Visual sources: NOAA Ocean Exploration (public domain) deep-sea photos and video where possible, Mixkit footage, Commons photos, US Navy public-domain photos for Indianapolis and Trieste, our animation for everything else.

## 1. Cold open

**Visual:** VFX: the surface of the Pacific from below, sun rays. A small figure sinks. A depth counter starts at 0 m. Quick flashes of what is coming: a fin, a huge eye, a glowing lure, a pale fish. Title. The three promises.

Somewhere above the Mariana Trench, you step off a boat and start to sink.

Below your feet there are almost eleven kilometres of water. You could drop Mount Everest in here and its peak would still be two kilometres under the waves.

On the way down, the light will go. The cold will come. And the pressure will grow until it is more than a thousand times what you feel right now.

But you will not be alone. Every few hundred metres, something new is waiting. Something built to hunt in that exact slice of the dark.

So what is waiting between the surface and the bottom of the deepest place on Earth?

On the way down, you will meet the shark that bit nuclear submarines. You will find the last fish before life gives up. And you will see what was waiting at the very bottom.

Let's sink.

## 2. The sunlit zone

**Visual:** Real footage: blue open ocean, sun rays, a shark cruising (Mixkit). Animation: the column of the ocean drawn as a tall strip with five zones, the diver a dot at the top. Counter 0–200 m.

The first two hundred metres are the sunlit zone. This is the ocean everyone knows. Warm, bright and blue.

It looks empty. Out here, far from any coast, there is nowhere to hide. No reefs. No rocks. No seabed. Only open water in every direction.

And that is exactly why the hunters here are so bold.

Out in the open ocean, food is rare. A predator that waits too long to check something out goes hungry. So the hunters of the open sea have learned to come straight to anything new.

Anything like you.

## 3. The open-ocean shark

**Visual:** Photo (NOAA, public domain): an oceanic whitetip with its long white-tipped fins. Animation: the shark circling the diver, closer each lap. Insert: USS Indianapolis (US Navy photo, public domain). Map insert: Tinian and the Philippine Sea.

The first to come is often the oceanic whitetip shark.

Long fins, tipped with white. A slow, confident swim. It does not rush. It circles, a little closer each time, and bumps what it is curious about.

In July 1945, in the sea west of here, it found a lot of people at once.

The cruiser USS Indianapolis had just delivered parts of the first atomic bomb to the island of Tinian, in the Mariana Islands. On the way back, a Japanese submarine hit it with two torpedoes. It sank in about twelve minutes.

Around nine hundred men went into the water. Nobody knew they were there.

For four days they floated in the open ocean, with no food and almost no water. And the sharks came. Survivors described them circling below, and taking the men at the edges of the groups.

When rescue finally came, by accident, three hundred and sixteen were still alive.

Most of the men did not die from sharks. They died from injuries, thirst, salt water and the sun. But for the survivors, the sharks were the part they never forgot.

## 4. The twilight zone

**Visual:** Animation: the water turns from blue to deep indigo, the counter passes 200 m. Real footage: a swordfish (Mixkit or NOAA). Diagram: a swordfish's head with a heater glowing behind the eye. Then a wall of small glowing fish rising at dusk.

Below two hundred metres, you enter the twilight zone.

There is still light here, but only a little. Blue, dim, and fading with every metre.

Here, the fast hunters have to see in the gloom.

The swordfish has a trick for that. Behind its eyes is a special organ, a kind of heater made from muscle. It warms its eyes and its brain to ten or fifteen degrees above the cold water around it.

Warm eyes are fast eyes. In the cold, dark water, a swordfish can still follow quick, darting prey that a cold-eyed fish would lose.

And there is a lot of prey to follow. Every night, billions of small fish, shrimp and squid swim up from the twilight zone to feed near the surface. And every morning, they sink back down.

It is the largest migration on Earth. It happens every single day, and almost nobody sees it.

## 5. The shark that bit submarines

**Visual:** Photo (NOAA or Commons, public domain/CC BY): the cookiecutter shark, small and cigar-shaped. Animation: its glowing belly with one dark band, a tuna coming to look, the shark latching on and twisting. Photo insert: a round scar on a whale or a fish. Drawn: a submarine's sonar dome with round bites.

Some of those migrating hunters are very small, and very strange.

This is the cookiecutter shark. It is about the length of your forearm. It spends its days deep down, and comes up at night.

Its belly glows. All of it, except for a dark band around its throat. From below, against the faint light above, that dark band may look like a small fish.

When a bigger hunter comes in to take it, the cookiecutter turns the tables. It latches on with its lips, sinks a ring of saw-like teeth into the skin, and spins. Then it swims away with a perfectly round plug of flesh.

Tuna, dolphins, seals, even great white sharks carry those round scars.

And in the 1970s, so did the United States Navy. Submarines in the Pacific came home with round bites in the rubber covers of their sonar domes. For a while, nobody could explain it. The answer was this little shark.

The Navy had to fit tougher covers. The shark that bit nuclear submarines was no longer than your arm.

## 6. The giant in the dark

**Visual:** Animation: deeper indigo, counter past 600 m. A huge eye opens in the dark. Our giant squid, long arms trailing. Map insert: the Ogasawara Islands, north of the Marianas. Real photo insert: the Ogasawara Islands (Commons). Diagram: the squid's eye compared with a dinner plate.

Deeper still, somewhere between five hundred and a thousand metres, lives something people searched for for more than a century.

The giant squid. It can be longer than a bus, most of that length in two long feeding arms. And it has the biggest eyes of any animal alive, each one about the size of a dinner plate.

For most of history, nobody had ever seen one alive. Only dead ones washed up on beaches.

Then, in 2004, a Japanese team led by Tsunemi Kubodera lowered a camera on a line, nine hundred metres down off the Ogasawara Islands, on the same chain of islands as the Marianas.

A giant squid came to the bait. The camera photographed it attacking, wrapping its arms around the line, and pulling. It fought for hours before it tore free and left one of its arms behind.

The first photos of a living giant squid in the wild came from the edge of this very ocean.

## 7. The thing that hunts the giant

**Visual:** Real footage: a sperm whale diving, tail up (Mixkit or NOAA). Animation: sound waves spreading through the dark from the whale's head and bouncing off a squid. Diagram: the whale's head, mostly a sound organ. Photo: a sperm whale's skin with round sucker scars (Commons, CC BY).

But even a giant has something to be afraid of.

Down here, in water with almost no light, the sperm whale hunts. It holds its breath, and dives more than a kilometre down, for close to an hour.

It does not need light. Its huge, square head is mostly a sound machine. It sends out clicks, some of the loudest sounds made by any animal, and listens for the echoes coming back from squid in the dark.

Sperm whales eat squid by the thousands. Inside their stomachs, scientists have found the beaks of giant squid.

And on their skin, they sometimes carry round scars from squid suckers. Marks of fights nobody has ever seen.

## 8. The midnight zone

**Visual:** Animation: the last blue light goes out at 1,000 m, the frame goes black except for drifting specks of light. Counter 1,000 m. NOAA photos (public domain): an anglerfish, a viperfish, a fangtooth. Our animation of a lure glowing in the black.

At one thousand metres, the sunlight is gone. All of it.

From here to the bottom, it is the midnight zone. Total darkness, forever. The only light is the light that animals make themselves.

And down here, light is a weapon.

The anglerfish carries a glowing lure on a rod growing from its head. Small animals swim toward the light, thinking it is food. Then the mouth opens.

The viperfish has teeth so long that they do not fit inside its mouth when it closes.

The fangtooth, a fish no bigger than your hand, has some of the largest teeth for its body of any fish in the sea.

Food is rare down here, so these hunters cannot afford to miss. Big mouths. Long teeth. Stretchy stomachs. Whatever they catch, they keep.

## 9. The invisible light

**Visual:** Animation: two glowing blue lights, then a red beam sweeping through the dark that only the dragonfish's eye can see. Photo (NOAA or Commons): a dragonfish. Diagram: a blue light seen by everyone, a red light seen by one.

Most animals in the deep make blue light, and almost all of them can only see blue.

The stoplight loosejaw, a kind of dragonfish, breaks that rule.

Under its eye it has a second light, and that one is red. Almost nothing in the deep can see red. To the shrimp and fish around it, that beam is completely invisible.

But the loosejaw can see it. Its eyes use a pigment made from chlorophyll, likely taken from the tiny animals it eats.

So it swims through the dark with its own secret searchlight, seeing its prey while its prey sees nothing.

## 10. The deepest-diving mammal

**Visual:** Real footage or photo: a Cuvier's beaked whale at the surface (NOAA, public domain). Animation: a line plunging down past the sperm whale's mark, counter racing to 2,992 m. Map insert: off southern California, where the whale was tagged.

But one mammal comes down even further.

In 2014, scientists published what tags on Cuvier's beaked whales had recorded off California. One whale dived to almost three thousand metres. Another stayed under for more than two hours on a single breath.

No mammal has been recorded deeper.

These whales come down here to hunt squid and fish in the dark, then climb all the way back up to breathe.

At three thousand metres, though, even they turn around. You keep sinking.

## 11. The abyss

**Visual:** Animation: the counter passes 4,000 m, a flat grey seabed of soft mud appears far below, then drops away into the trench. NOAA photos (public domain): a rattail fish, a sea cucumber walking on the mud. Animation: a whale carcass on the seabed, sharks and hagfish arriving.

Past four thousand metres is the abyss.

The water is just above freezing. The pressure is four hundred times what it is at the surface. And food is so rare that most animals here live on what falls from above. A steady snow of dead plankton, called marine snow.

So the hunters of the abyss are patient. Long, slow fish called rattails cruise just above the mud, waiting for the smell of anything dead or dying.

When a whale dies and sinks, it is a feast that can last for years. Sleeper sharks, hagfish and crabs arrive from far away, following the smell down through the dark.

Then, just past six thousand metres, the seabed drops away under you.

This is the trench.

## 12. The hadal zone

**Visual:** Animation: steep trench walls on both sides, the counter past 6,000 m. NOAA footage or photos (public domain): amphipods swarming a bait trap. Diagram: amphipods drawn at true size, from a grain of rice to longer than a hand.

Below six thousand metres is the hadal zone, named after Hades, the Greek world of the dead.

It is not dead.

Lower a piece of bait to the bottom here, and in minutes it is covered in amphipods. Pale, shrimp-like scavengers, swarming in their thousands. Most are small. Some deep-sea amphipods grow longer than your hand.

They find food by smell. In a place where nothing falls often, they cannot afford to be slow.

And where there is a swarm of food, there is a hunter.

## 13. The last fish

**Visual:** NOAA or JAMSTEC-style photo where licence allows; otherwise our animation of the Mariana snailfish, pale pink and almost see-through, hovering over a swarm of amphipods. Counter passes 8,000 m. Insert: 8,178 m (2017) and 8,336 m (2023, Izu-Ogasawara Trench). Diagram: a fish body filling with a pressure-protecting molecule.

At around eight thousand metres, you meet the last fish.

It is not a monster. It is the Mariana snailfish. Small, pink, soft, and almost see-through. It looks like a tadpole that has been left in water too long.

But down here, it is the top predator. It hovers over the amphipod swarms and sucks them in.

In 2017, a snailfish in the Mariana Trench was filmed at eight thousand one hundred and seventy-eight metres, a record at the time. In 2023, another snailfish was filmed at eight thousand three hundred and thirty-six metres, in a trench just to the north. It is the deepest fish ever filmed.

And it may be about as deep as a fish can go.

Fish protect their bodies from pressure with a molecule called TMAO. The deeper they live, the more they need. Scientists have calculated that, at around eight thousand two hundred to eight thousand four hundred metres, a fish would need so much of it that water would start flooding into its body.

So, around eight and a half kilometres down, the fish stop.

## 14. Below the fish

**Visual:** Animation: the counter keeps climbing, 9,000 m, 10,000 m. The frame is nearly empty. A single amphipod drifts past the lens. A pressure gauge drawn as a tiny car on a fingertip.

But the trench keeps going. Two and a half more kilometres of water.

Down here, the pressure is like the weight of a small car pressing on every fingertip.

There are no fish. But there is still life. Amphipods have been collected from the very bottom of the deepest point. Sea cucumbers, worms, and single-celled giants the size of a fist live in the mud.

They do not hunt in any way you would recognise. But they eat, and they survive, in a place where almost nothing should.

## 15. The fish that wasn't

**Visual:** Real photos (US Navy, public domain): the bathyscaphe Trieste; Jacques Piccard and Don Walsh. Animation: the Trieste sinking, a cloud of mud around its window, a vague flat shape. A MYTH stamp.

The first people to see this place came down in 1960.

Jacques Piccard and Don Walsh rode the bathyscaphe Trieste to the bottom of the Challenger Deep, the deepest point of the trench. It took them almost five hours to sink.

When they landed, Piccard reported seeing a flat fish, like a sole, on the bottom.

It became one of the most famous sightings in the history of the deep. But fish are not thought to be able to live that deep, and the window was clouded with mud stirred up by the landing.

Walsh later said they might have been mistaken. Whatever they saw, it was almost certainly not a fish.

## 16. What was waiting at the bottom

**Visual:** Animation: the counter slows and stops at almost 11,000 m. Soft grey mud. Our camera light sweeps across it and finds a pale, crumpled shape. It is a plastic shopping bag. Insert: the year 1998 and the depth, 10,898 m.

So what is waiting at the very bottom of the deepest place on Earth?

Mud. Cold, soft, grey mud. Water just above freezing. And complete darkness that has lasted for millions of years.

And in 1998, a Japanese robot sub filmed something else lying on the seabed, at ten thousand eight hundred and ninety-eight metres.

A plastic shopping bag.

It is the deepest piece of plastic ever recorded. Scientists found it while going through thirty years of deep-sea video. It was there before most of the people watching this were born.

We have sent more people into space than to the bottom of this trench. But our rubbish got there first.

## 17. Close

**Visual:** Animation: the whole column again, from the surface to the bottom, each hunter lit up in its zone in turn, then the camera rises back to the blue surface. End card.

So here is everything waiting on the way down.

At the surface, the sharks that come straight to anything new.

In the twilight, the swordfish with warm eyes, and the little shark that bit submarines.

In the dark, the giant squid, and the whale that hunts it with sound.

In the midnight zone, lures, invisible light and teeth too long to close.

In the abyss, the patient hunters that wait for food to fall.

And at the bottom of the hunting world, eight kilometres down, a small pink fish that rules a kingdom of shrimp.

Below it, no more fish. Only mud, darkness, and one plastic bag.

The deeper you go, the smaller the hunters get. But they never stop hunting.
