## 1. Cold open

**Visual:** VFX: under the ice, light rays, drifting particles. A huge spotted shape rising toward the camera. Insert (drawn portrait): the photographer. Flash-forwards: a penguin on the water, an ice edge, a snorkeller's fins. Title at about 12 s, then the three promises.

Antarctica. 2006.

A photographer slips into the freezing water under the ice, alone, with his camera.

The water is just below freezing. The light fades fast. All he can hear is his own breathing.

Out of the dark comes a female leopard seal. Three and a half metres long. Heavier than a horse.

She swims straight at him. And she opens her mouth around his camera, and his head.

So what happens when a leopard seal decides what you are? Why does it look like it's smiling? Has it ever killed a person? And why would a killer like this try to feed a human?

## 2. The face

**Visual:** Real footage of a leopard seal at the surface (Mixkit/Commons). Animation: the head in profile, the long jaw opening wide, the upturned corners of the mouth. Insert: a map of Antarctica with the pack ice lit. Scale shot: seal against a person and a horse.

This is the leopard seal.

It lives on the pack ice around Antarctica, and it is the only seal known to hunt other seals.

Its head looks less like a seal's and more like a reptile's. Long. Flat. Built around its jaws.

Its name comes from its coat. Dark grey on the back, and pale on the throat and sides, covered in dark spots, like a leopard.

The females are bigger than the males, which is rare for a mammal. A large female can be more than three and a half metres long and weigh more than five hundred kilograms.

It hunts alone. It does not need anyone.

And when it opens its mouth, it opens it very, very wide.

## 3. The teeth

**Visual:** Animation: a skull cutaway, the long canines glowing, then the cheek teeth: three-pointed, interlocking. VFX: krill drawn into the mouth with suction lines, water pushing out of the corners. Insert (photo, Commons): a leopard seal skull.

At the front, it has long canine teeth, made for gripping and killing.

But behind them is something stranger.

Its cheek teeth have three sharp points each, and they lock together like the teeth of a comb.

Scientists in Australia watched leopard seals feed and found what they were for.

The seal sucks in a mouthful of water full of krill, and pushes the water back out through those teeth. The krill stay inside.

In some places, krill make up most of what it eats.

So this is an animal that can strain food as small as a paperclip, and kill prey as big as a seal. With the same mouth.

## 4. The smile

**Visual:** Close animation of the face, the mouth corners rising into what looks like a grin. A big "IT'S NOT A SMILE" stamp. Real footage: a leopard seal head at the surface. Promise 1 answered on screen.

Here is the answer to the first question.

The corners of a leopard seal's mouth curve upwards. From the front, it looks like it's smiling at you.

It isn't.

That shape is just how its jaw is built. It has nothing to do with how it feels about you.

People who work in Antarctica learn to read the rest of the animal instead. Where it is looking. How close it is. Whether it keeps following.

Because that friendly face is the last thing many animals ever see.

## 5. The ice edge

**Visual:** VFX: underwater view up at the ice edge, penguins' feet above. Animation: the seal waiting, almost invisible, then the strike. Real footage: penguins at the ice edge. A diagram of the thrash.

Its favourite place is the edge of the ice.

Penguins have to cross it twice a day. Out to sea to feed, and back again.

The penguins seem to know the danger. They crowd at the edge, hesitating, waiting for someone else to go first.

The leopard seal waits just under the edge, almost completely hidden.

When a penguin jumps in, it strikes from below.

Then it does something brutal. It grabs the bird and whips it against the surface of the water, again and again, until the skin comes away.

It is fast. It is patient. And it hunts the place where the ice meets the water.

Which is exactly where people stand, too.

## 6. Shackleton's men, 1916

**Visual:** History grade. Photo inserts (public domain, Frank Hurley): Shackleton's men on the ice, the camp on the floe, a portrait of Thomas Orde-Lees, a portrait of Frank Wild. Animation: a man on skis, a dark head bursting through the ice behind him. Map: the Weddell Sea.

In 1916, the men of Ernest Shackleton's expedition were stranded on the sea ice after their ship, the Endurance, had been crushed.

One of them, Thomas Orde-Lees, was out on the ice alone, looking for food.

A leopard seal burst out of the water and went after him.

It chased him across the ice. He ran. It dived under the ice, and burst up again right in front of him.

He screamed for help.

Frank Wild, the expedition's second-in-command, ran out with a rifle and shot it, about nine metres from him.

The men had travelled to the bottom of the world. And one of them had nearly been taken by the thing that lived under it.

## 7. Under the ice

**Visual:** VFX: dark water, torchlight, a long spotted body sliding past. Real footage: a diver under ice (Mixkit, labelled illustrative). Animation: the seal following a diver at a distance. Insert: Rothera Research Station photo (Commons).

For a hundred years, almost every meeting between people and leopard seals happened on the ice.

Then people started to dive in Antarctica. Scientists, photographers, film crews.

Under the water, the leopard seal is in its own world. It is faster than you. It sees better than you. And it can stay down longer than you.

To a seal, a diver is slow, clumsy and loud. Every breath sends a stream of bubbles to the surface. Every kick stirs the water.

Many divers say the same thing. They did not see it arrive. It was just there, beside them, watching.

Some seals swim away. Some follow. And some come closer, and closer, to find out what you are.

That is what happened to the photographer.

## 8. Four days with a predator

**Visual:** Drawn portrait insert: Paul Nicklen. Animation: the seal circling a diver with a camera, opening her jaws around it. Real footage: a leopard seal underwater (Commons/Mixkit). A day counter: 1, 2, 3, 4.

His name is Paul Nicklen, and he was in Antarctica to photograph leopard seals for National Geographic.

When the big female first swam at him, he thought he might die.

She opened her jaws around his camera, and around his head. Then she let go.

And then she swam away, and came back with a penguin.

She let it go in front of him. Alive.

When he did nothing, she brought another one.

For four days, every time he got into the water, she brought him penguins. Live ones that swam away. Then dead ones, pushed right up against his camera.

She seemed frustrated that he could not catch anything.

When he kept refusing, she blew bubbles in his face, and swam past him with another penguin, as if to show him how it was done.

He came out of the water with some of the most famous wildlife photographs ever taken. And a story no one had heard before.

## 9. Why feed a human?

**Visual:** Animation: the seal watching the diver "fail" to catch a penguin, a thought line. VFX: the camera lens reflecting her face. Promise 3 answered on screen. Text: THEORY, NOT FACT.

So why would a predator feed a human?

No one knows for sure.

One idea is that she saw a clumsy, slow creature in her water, one that could not hunt, and tried to help it.

Another is that she was teaching it, the way a mother seal shows a young one how to hunt.

Nicklen himself said it felt like she had decided he was useless, and was trying to keep him alive.

Whatever it was, it shows something important about this animal. It is not a machine. It makes choices.

And not every choice goes the photographer's way.

## 10. Gareth Wood, 1985

**Visual:** History grade. Drawn portrait insert: Gareth Wood. Map: Ross Island, Cape Evans. Animation: three men crossing thin sea ice, a head bursting through, the jaws closing on a leg, crampons kicking. Insert (Commons): Scott's hut at Cape Evans.

In 1985, a British expedition was following the route of Captain Scott, the explorer who died in Antarctica in 1912.

One of them, Gareth Wood, was walking across thin sea ice.

A leopard seal came up through the edge of the ice, seized his leg, and began to drag him toward the water.

It let go, then bit him again.

His companions kicked it in the head, again and again, with the steel spikes of their crampons, until it finally let him go.

He survived, with deep wounds in his leg.

For eighty years, the story had been the same. Close calls. Scares. Lucky escapes.

Then, in 2003, it changed.

## 11. Rothera, 2003

**Visual:** Insert photos (Commons): Rothera Research Station, Ryder Bay. Drawn portrait insert: Kirsty Brown. Animation: two snorkellers in a calm bay, a boat nearby. No graphic depiction of the attack: the water, a fin, then the boat.

Kirsty Brown was twenty-eight, a marine biologist with the British Antarctic Survey, working at Rothera Research Station.

On the twenty-second of July 2003, she went snorkelling in the bay with a colleague, part of a survey of the sea floor.

A leopard seal that no one had seen came up and took hold of her.

Her partner saw her under the water, with the seal holding on to her.

The seal let her go near the boat, and the team pulled her out and tried everything to save her.

She did not survive.

## 12. The only one

**Visual:** Real footage: calm Antarctic water and ice (Mixkit, labelled illustrative). Insert photo (Commons): Kirsty Island on a map. A timeline from 1916 to today with a single red mark at 2003. Promise 2 answered on screen.

Here is the answer to the second question.

Yes, a leopard seal has killed a person. Once.

Kirsty Brown's death is the only known fatal leopard seal attack.

Scientists who studied it found something else unusual. The seal attacked at the surface, and nobody had seen it before it struck.

Afterwards, the British Antarctic Survey brought in new safety rules for diving and snorkelling.

And a small island near Rothera was named after her. Kirsty Island.

One death in more than a century of people working beside these animals. But it showed what could happen.

## 13. Not only in Antarctica

**Visual:** Map: leopard seal sightings in New Zealand, Australia, South America. Photo insert (Commons, if CC): Owha in Auckland. Real footage: a marina. Animation: a seal hauled out on a pontoon, people at a distance, a 20 m line.

And you do not have to go to Antarctica to meet one.

Leopard seals turn up on the coasts of New Zealand, Australia, South Africa and South America.

In New Zealand, one female, named Owha, lived for years around the marinas and beaches of Auckland.

Scientists there have identified more than two hundred different leopard seals visiting the country, and the government now counts them as residents, not visitors.

They haul out on boat ramps and pontoons, a few metres from where people walk their dogs.

Some have even given birth there. Researchers have recorded leopard seal pups born on New Zealand's coast.

The country has a phone line just for leopard seal sightings, so scientists can track every visitor.

So in some places, the leopard seal is not at the end of the world. It is at the end of the jetty.

## 14. What it eats

**Visual:** Animation: a list of prey icons appearing one by one: krill, fish, squid, penguins, other seals. Real footage: fur seals, penguins. VFX: a seal pup at the ice edge, a shadow below.

Look at what it hunts, and you understand why people take it seriously.

Krill. Fish. Squid. Penguins.

And other seals. Young crabeater seals and fur seals, much bigger than a penguin.

Up to three in every four crabeater seals that survive their first year carry scars from leopard seal attacks. Long, parallel wounds, from the year they were young enough to catch.

The biggest females take the biggest, richest prey.

A leopard seal can live for more than twenty-six years. That is a long time to learn the ice edges, the colonies, and the places where food comes to the water.

And to learn what a human is.

## 15. What it decides

**Visual:** Animation: the seal circling a diver, three outcomes branching: swims away, follows, approaches. The three promises ticked. VFX: the lens reflection again.

So what really happens when a leopard seal decides what you are?

Most of the time, it decides you are not food. It watches you, and it leaves.

Sometimes, it decides you are something to investigate. It follows. It tests you. It might even bring you a penguin.

And very, very rarely, it decides you are prey.

Photographers, scientists and divers who have spent years with them all say the same thing. You cannot always tell which seal is which. The curious one and the dangerous one can look exactly alike.

The problem is that from the outside, those three look exactly the same at first. The same face. The same smile.

## 16. If you meet one

**Visual:** A checklist, one drawn example per line: stay out of the water, keep 20 m back, never get between it and the sea, never stand at the ice edge, leave the water slowly. Insert: New Zealand Department of Conservation guidance (text only, no logo).

So here is how you stay on the right side of that decision.

If a leopard seal is in the water, stay out of it.

On land, keep at least twenty metres away. That is the rule in New Zealand.

Never stand between a seal and the sea. That is its way out, and you are in the way.

Never feed one, and never let a dog near one. A seal that learns to come close to people is a seal that will one day get too close.

Be careful at the ice edge. That is its hunting ground.

And if one comes toward you in the water, do not panic and do not splash. Get out, calmly and quickly.

It is not a monster. But it is the top predator of its world, and you are a visitor in it.

## 17. Close

**Visual:** VFX: the opening shot again, under the ice, the huge spotted shape. This time the diver backs away and climbs out onto the ice. The seal surfaces, looks, and sinks. End card.

So you stop swimming toward it. You turn, slowly, and kick for the hole in the ice.

One metre. Two. Your hands find the edge, and you pull yourself out onto the ice.

Behind you, the water is still.

Then a long head rises, just at the edge. It looks at you. That wide mouth, curved up at the corners.

It is not smiling.

It is deciding.

And today, it decides to let you go.
