## 1. Cold open

**Visual:** VFX: dark Arctic water under ice, a huge grey shape drifting. Photo: the Mayflower II replica. Animation: a timeline running from 1620 to today along the shark's body. Title. The three promises.

In 1620, a ship called the Mayflower crossed the Atlantic.

Somewhere in the cold, dark water of the North Atlantic, there may be a shark that was already alive when it did.

It is still swimming today. Slowly. In the dark. It has outlived every person who was alive when it was born.

This is the Greenland shark. And scientists think it may live for around four hundred years.

But how can anyone know the age of a shark? And how does an animal live that long in one of the harshest places on Earth?

In this video, you will find out how a shark's age is hidden in its eye. How something this slow catches seals. And what its body knows about growing old.

## 2. The sleeper

**Visual:** Real photos: a Greenland shark close to the camera, grey-brown, small eyes (iNaturalist / Openverse). Map: the North Atlantic and the Arctic, Greenland to Norway. Animation: the shark beside a person and a small car. Real footage: sea ice (Mixkit).

The Greenland shark lives in the coldest seas on the planet. Around Greenland, Iceland, Norway and Canada, and down into the deep North Atlantic.

It belongs to a family called sleeper sharks. And it looks the part.

It is thick and heavy, grey-brown, with a blunt head and tiny eyes. Big ones grow longer than five metres. Bigger than a car.

It spends most of its life in water close to freezing, from near the surface under the ice to more than two kilometres down.

Few people have ever seen one alive. Most of what we know comes from sharks caught by accident, by fishermen, and from cameras dropped into the deep.

And it grows unbelievably slowly. Around one centimetre a year.

## 3. A shark with no clock

**Visual:** Animation: a vertebra from a great white with growth rings; then a soft, ringless Greenland shark vertebra. A clock with no hands. Photo: a scientist measuring a shark on deck (Openverse, labelled).

Scientists have always struggled to age sharks.

For most sharks, they count growth rings in the vertebrae, the discs of the spine. One ring, roughly one year. Like a tree.

But the Greenland shark's vertebrae are soft. They have no clear rings to count. Its fins and skin give no clue either.

So for a long time, nobody could say how old a Greenland shark was. Only that it grew very slowly, and must be very old.

Then, a team of scientists led by a Danish biologist named Julius Nielsen found a clock that had been hiding in plain sight.

## 4. The eye

**Visual:** Animation: a cutaway of the shark's eye; the lens; layers like an onion; the centre glowing. Real photo: a Greenland shark's eye up close.

The clock was in the eye.

The lens of a shark's eye grows in layers, like an onion. New layers are added on the outside all through life.

But the very centre of the lens, the nucleus, is made before the shark is born. And it never changes again. The proteins inside it stay exactly as they were, for the whole of the shark's life.

So if you could date the centre of the lens, you would know when the shark was born.

And there is a way to date it. With carbon.

## 5. The bomb pulse

**Visual:** Archive: a 1950s US nuclear test (public domain). Animation: a graph of carbon-14 in the air spiking in the early 1960s, then the carbon flowing into the sea, into fish, into sharks. Lens centres lit "before" and "after".

In the 1950s and early 1960s, countries tested nuclear bombs in the open air.

Those tests did something strange to the planet. They nearly doubled the amount of a kind of carbon, carbon-14, in the air.

That extra carbon washed into the oceans, into plankton, into fish, and into the animals that eat them. Everything that grew after the early 1960s carries a mark of it. Scientists call it the bomb pulse.

When the team tested the eye lenses of twenty-eight Greenland sharks, only the smallest ones, about two metres long or less, carried the bomb mark.

Every larger shark was born before the bombs.

That one clue gave the team a fixed point. A two-metre shark was born around the early 1960s. So anything much bigger had to be much older.

Using the carbon in the lenses, and how fast these sharks grow, they worked out how old the big ones must be.

## 6. Three hundred and ninety-two

**Visual:** Animation: the 28 sharks lined up by length; the biggest at 5.02 m; a number counter climbing to 392 with a "± 120" band. Card: AT LEAST 272 YEARS. Card: MATURE AT ≈ 156.

The results came out in 2016. They shocked the world.

The biggest shark in the study, a female just over five metres long, was estimated to be about three hundred and ninety-two years old.

The estimate is not exact. It could be as much as a hundred and twenty years less, or more. But even at the low end, she was at least two hundred and seventy-two.

Think about what that means. If she really was three hundred and ninety-two, she was born around the year 1624. She was already swimming when Isaac Newton was born. She was alive through the whole history of the United States.

That made the Greenland shark the longest-living animal with a backbone ever found.

And there was something even stranger. Females only seem to be ready to have young when they are about four metres long. By the team's estimate, that takes around a hundred and fifty years.

A hundred and fifty years, just to grow up.

## 7. Life in slow motion

**Visual:** Animation: the shark gliding with a slow tail beat; a counter for one tail beat every seven seconds; a human walking beside it at the same speed. Real footage: ice and dark water.

How does an animal live that long? Part of the answer is that it lives slowly.

In 2012, scientists put tags on Greenland sharks to measure how they swim. On average, they cruised at about a third of a metre per second. Slower than a gentle stroll.

Their tails swept from side to side only about once every seven seconds.

For their size, they were the slowest fish ever measured.

In water this cold, everything in its body runs slowly. Its heart. Its digestion. Its growth. And maybe even its ageing.

## 8. The slow hunter

**Visual:** Animation: a sleeping seal hanging in the water, a dark shape rising slowly beneath it. Real photos: a ringed seal / harbour seal (iNaturalist). Stamp-style list: MOOSE · POLAR BEAR · HORSE · REINDEER.

But here is the mystery. Something this slow should go hungry.

And yet, inside Greenland sharks, scientists have found seals. Fresh ones. Some eaten whole.

Seals are fast. Much faster than a Greenland shark. So how does it catch them?

One idea is that it does not chase them at all. In the Arctic, seals often sleep in the water, to stay safe from polar bears on the ice. A sleeping seal, drifting in the dark, may never see a slow shape rising from below.

The Greenland shark also eats whatever it finds. Its stomachs have held fish, squid, and the remains of moose, horses, reindeer, and even polar bears. In one case, an entire reindeer.

Nobody has ever filmed a Greenland shark catching a seal. How it does it is still a mystery.

## 9. The eyes that should not see

**Visual:** Real photo: a Greenland shark with a pale copepod hanging from its eye (iNaturalist / Openverse). Animation: the parasite on the cornea, scratches. Then the retina in cross-section, rods only, still lit.

Look closely at a Greenland shark, and you will often see something hanging from its eye.

It is a parasite, a small crustacean about three centimetres long. It clamps onto the surface of the eye and stays there. It scars the eye as it feeds.

For years, people assumed most Greenland sharks were half blind.

Then, in 2025, scientists studied the eyes of Greenland sharks more than a hundred years old.

The retinas were healthy. No widespread damage, no signs of breaking down with age. Built only for dim light, with no colour vision at all. But still working.

After a century or more in the dark, these sharks can still see.

## 10. What its body knows

**Visual:** Animation: a DNA double helix stretching; a counter: 6.5 BILLION letters; "jumping genes" hopping along the strand; repair tools stitching breaks. Comparison bar: human genome vs Greenland shark genome.

So what is its secret?

In 2024, scientists read the Greenland shark's whole genome for the first time.

It is huge. About six and a half billion letters of DNA. Twice as long as ours.

More than seventy percent of it is made of so-called jumping genes, pieces of DNA that copy themselves and move around.

Usually, that kind of chaos would cause damage. But the Greenland shark seems to have extra copies of genes that repair DNA.

In its eyes, too, the 2025 study found active repair genes.

The idea is that, over hundreds of years, its body keeps fixing itself. Not perfectly, but well enough.

Scientists hope it could one day help us understand ageing in other animals, including us. Why some bodies wear out in decades, and one shark's body lasts for centuries.

## 11. Poison meat

**Visual:** Real photos: hákarl drying in a shed in Iceland (Openverse); cubes of hákarl on a plate. Animation: a chemical label TMAO.

There is one more thing that makes this shark strange. Its meat is poisonous.

To survive in deep, cold water, its body is full of a chemical called TMAO, which helps protect its proteins from the pressure and the cold.

But for people, and for dogs, fresh Greenland shark meat can cause sickness that looks like being very drunk.

In Iceland, people found a way around it. The meat is left to ferment for weeks, then hung to dry for months.

The result is called hákarl. It smells strongly of ammonia. And it is still eaten today.

## 12. Far from home

**Visual:** Map: from the Arctic to the Caribbean. Real photo: Glover's Reef, Belize (Openverse). Animation: warm water above, a cold layer far below, a grey shark gliding in it.

In April 2022, a team of fishermen and a researcher were tagging tiger sharks off Belize, in the Caribbean.

From the deep slope of Glover's Reef, at night, they pulled up something no one expected. A huge, slow, grey shark with tiny eyes.

It was most likely a Greenland shark, or a hybrid with its Pacific cousin. Thousands of kilometres from the Arctic.

How? The surface there is warm. But the reef drops away steeply, from about five hundred metres to nearly three kilometres deep. And far below the warm surface, the water is cold, all year round. Only a few degrees above freezing.

Greenland sharks may be living much further south than anyone knew. Not in the Arctic sea, but in the cold, dark layer beneath the warm one.

## 13. What we still do not know

**Visual:** Animation: question marks over a map of the deep North Atlantic; a female shark gliding away into darkness. Card: WHERE ARE THEY BORN?

For an animal this famous, we know very little.

Even the four-hundred-year figure is still an estimate. It depends on how fast these sharks really grow, and that has never been measured over a whole lifetime. Nobody has been around long enough.

Scientists still do not know exactly where Greenland sharks give birth, or how often. Their young are almost never seen.

We do not know how many there are. We do not know how far they travel in a lifetime, or what really happens in their brain, their heart and their muscles that lets them last for centuries.

Every answer so far has raised a new question.

## 14. Too slow to replace

**Visual:** Real footage: a fishing trawler in the North Atlantic (Mixkit, labelled). Animation: a net pulling a Greenland shark up; a counter: 150 YEARS TO MATURITY. Card: IUCN · VULNERABLE.

And there is a danger in living slowly.

Every year, Greenland sharks are caught by accident in fishing nets and on lines in the North Atlantic.

For most fish, that can be replaced. But a Greenland shark that dies today may have taken more than a century to grow up. Its population could take centuries to recover.

That is why the Greenland shark is now listed as vulnerable.

## 15. Close

**Visual:** Animation: the old female drifting under the ice; the 1620 → today timeline glowing along her body. Real footage: Arctic ice at dusk. End card.

Right now, somewhere in the dark under the Arctic ice, a Greenland shark is drifting slowly through water near freezing.

She has no idea how old she is.

She was here before the Mayflower, perhaps. Before steam engines, before electric light, before anyone took a photograph.

She has seen the sea change around her, slowly, for centuries.

And if we let her, she may still be swimming long after all of us are gone.
