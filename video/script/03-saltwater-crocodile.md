# What Happens When a Saltwater Crocodile Sees You First

Draft 1. Target length: about 12 minutes. Style: dive computer (see CLAUDE.md).
Thumbnail text: IT SAW YOU FIRST

Each scene has narration and a visual note. Evidence labels shown on screen are given in the visual note:
LOGGED = measured and sourced, REPORTED = credible but secondhand, MYTH = disputed or false.

---

## 1. Hook

**Visual:** Through the mask at dusk, waist-deep in brown water at a river mouth. Instruments: depth 0.9 m, visibility 1 m. Far off, two bumps on the surface. They do not move. Then they sink. Title and the three promises.

You are standing waist-deep in warm, brown water. A river mouth in northern Australia, just before dark.

You can see about one metre. After that, nothing.

The surface is flat. No splashes. No birds. It feels safe.

Twenty metres away, two small bumps sit on the water. They look like a floating branch.

They are eyes.

They have been pointed at you since you stepped off the bank. It knew you were here before you were even wet.

And now, without a single ripple, the bumps sink.

You never saw it. It saw you first. So what happens next?

## 2. The animal

**Visual:** Scan mode: the animal identified. Length bar growing past a human, a car, a small boat. Real photo of a large male. Lolong's numbers. LOGGED: Guinness World Records (Lolong); REPORTED: typical adult size.

This is the saltwater crocodile. The largest reptile alive.

A big male is four to five metres long. Longer than most cars.

He weighs around half a tonne. Some weigh twice that.

The biggest ever held in captivity was called Lolong. He was caught in the Philippines in 2011.

Six point one seven metres long. One thousand and seventy-five kilograms.

That is a predator as long as a minibus, heavier than a horse, and able to hide in water that barely covers your knees.

And his design has hardly changed since the dinosaurs.

## 3. Armour

**Visual:** Cutaway of the skin: bony plates lighting up along the back. Then the stomach: stones inside it, and a bone dissolving. REPORTED: standard crocodilian anatomy references.

And it is built to last.

Under the skin of its back lie rows of bony plates. Living armour, fused into the hide.

They protect it. They also soak up heat from the sun, like panels.

Inside, its stomach is one of the most acidic of any animal. It dissolves bone, horn, and shell.

It even swallows stones, and keeps them there. They help grind what the acid cannot.

Nothing it eats goes to waste. And nothing about it is fragile.

## 4. How it saw you

**Visual:** Scan of the head. Eyes, ears and nostrils light up along one line at the surface. Night view with eyeshine. Then the jaw: thousands of dots, ripples spreading from your legs and reaching them. LOGGED: Leitch & Catania, Journal of Experimental Biology, 2012.

Start with how it found you.

Its eyes, ears and nostrils all sit on top of its head, in one line. It can see, hear and breathe with almost nothing showing.

At night, its eyes gather light yours cannot. In a torch beam they glow red.

But it does not need to see you at all.

Its jaws are covered in thousands of tiny black dots. Each one is a pressure sensor.

Together, they are more sensitive than your fingertips.

Every step you take sends a ripple through the water. It feels each one.

It knows where you are. It knows how big you are. It knows which way you are walking.

## 5. It can wait

**Visual:** A breath timer for you against one for it. A heart monitor slowing to a few beats. A calendar flipping months with no food. REPORTED: Northern Territory Government crocodile guide; PBS Nature.

Now it does the thing it is best at. It waits.

You can hold your breath for a minute, maybe two.

It can stay under for ten to fifteen minutes without effort. Resting on the bottom, far longer.

While it waits, its heart slows down. To just a few beats a minute.

It is in no hurry to eat, either. A large crocodile can go for months without a meal.

So it has air. It has energy. It has time.

You are the only one here who is running out of anything.

## 6. The strike

**Visual:** A ruler from the water's edge: the strike zone. The animal drawn under the surface, tail coiled, then launching. A reaction-time bar: its strike against your blink. REPORTED: Northern Territory and Queensland "Be Crocwise" guidance.

When it moves, it moves underwater. No wake. No sound.

It closes the distance until it is one body length away.

Then the tail does the work. One sweep, and half a tonne leaves the water.

It is over faster than you can step back.

This is why the official advice in crocodile country is always the same. The danger is not the deep water.

It is the edge. The last few metres of shallows, and the bank beside them.

That is where it hunts. That is where you are standing.

## 7. The bite

**Visual:** A force gauge climbing: you, a lion, then the crocodile, off the scale. The number. Then the weak point: a hand holding the jaws shut. LOGGED: Erickson and others, PLoS ONE, 2012.

Then the jaws close.

In 2012, scientists measured the bite of every crocodile species on Earth.

The saltwater crocodile won. Sixteen thousand four hundred newtons.

It is the strongest bite ever measured in a living animal. More than ten times the force of your own.

Its teeth are not built to cut. They are built to hold. More than sixty of them, and when one breaks, another grows in.

There is one strange weakness. The muscles that open its mouth are feeble. A person could hold those jaws shut with their hands.

But that only helps before it bites. Not after.

## 8. The death roll

**Visual:** A top-down diagram of the roll, spinning. A counter: roll one, two, three. Then a map pin on Kakadu and the year 1985. LOGGED: Val Plumwood's own account, "Being Prey".

It cannot chew. So it does something else.

It grips, and it spins. Its whole body, around and around, in the water.

This is the death roll. It is meant to pull you off your feet, drag you under, and drown you.

In 1985, a woman named Val Plumwood was paddling a canoe alone in Kakadu when a crocodile took her.

It rolled her once. She came up and breathed.

It rolled her again.

And a third time.

Then its grip slipped. She dragged herself up a mud bank, and walked and crawled for hours to be found.

She lived. Three death rolls, and she lived.

## 9. It crosses oceans

**Visual:** A map of the Indian and Pacific oceans, the range lighting up. A tracked animal moving along a coast, a day counter and a distance counter. A cutaway of salt glands on the tongue. LOGGED: Campbell and others, Journal of Animal Ecology, 2010.

And do not think it is trapped in this river.

The name is a clue. This crocodile goes to sea.

It lives from India, across Southeast Asia, to northern Australia and the islands beyond.

Scientists tracked one male by satellite. He swam five hundred and ninety kilometres in twenty-five days.

He was not swimming hard. He was riding the ocean currents, like a surfer waiting for the right wave.

Glands on its tongue push out the extra salt.

So a beach is not safe just because it is a beach. In its range, any warm coast can have one.

## 10. Can you outrun it?

**Visual:** A split screen. Left: a zigzag path with a red MYTH stamp. Right: a straight line away from the water, with the animal stopping after a few metres. MYTH: the zigzag rule; REPORTED: crocodile specialists.

So could you simply run?

You may have heard the rule. If a crocodile chases you, run in a zigzag.

That is a myth.

On land, a big crocodile is quick for a few metres. Then it is finished. It is built to explode out of the water, not to chase.

A healthy adult can outrun it. So do not waste steps turning.

Run straight. Run away from the water. Do not stop until you are well up the bank.

The problem was never the chase. It is the first second. And in that second, it is faster than you.

## 11. The myth

**Visual:** A wartime report stamped with a number, then a red MYTH stamp across it. The single source highlighted. MYTH: no official British or Japanese record; one secondhand account.

There is a famous story about these animals.

In 1945, on Ramree Island, a thousand soldiers retreated into a mangrove swamp at night.

By morning, the story goes, crocodiles had eaten nine hundred of them.

It was once listed as the worst animal attack in history.

But it comes from one man, who did not see it happen. No army record, on either side, mentions it.

Experts doubt there were ever enough crocodiles in that swamp to do it.

Most historians now call it a myth.

The real numbers are smaller. And they are bad enough.

## 12. The real toll

**Visual:** A counter of attacks and deaths for one country in one year. A bar chart of the same. Then a flat line for the Northern Territory after education began. LOGGED: CrocAttack database, 2024; Charles Darwin University study.

Here is what is actually recorded.

In Indonesia, in 2024 alone, there were one hundred and seventy-nine crocodile attacks on people.

Ninety-two of them were fatal.

That is one country, in one year. And researchers believe many more are never reported.

Many victims were not tourists. They were people fishing, washing, or fetching water. People at the edge.

In northern Australia, attacks are far rarer. Not because the crocodiles are different.

Because of warning signs, removal of problem animals, and people who know the rules.

## 13. We nearly erased it

**Visual:** A population counter falling through the 1950s and 60s to a low point, a "PROTECTED 1971" marker, then climbing. Real photo of a warning sign. LOGGED: Northern Territory Government; Journal of Wildlife Management, 2011.

For a while, though, we were winning.

After the Second World War, crocodile skin was worth a fortune. Hunters shot them by the tens of thousands.

By 1971, in Australia's Northern Territory, about three thousand were left.

Then they were protected.

Today there are around one hundred thousand.

The population has almost fully recovered. And there are far more people living, fishing, and swimming beside them.

The animal came back. The rules for living next to it had to come back too.

## 14. The other side

**Visual:** A mound nest on a riverbank, a thermometer deciding male or female. The mother's jaws opening gently with hatchlings inside. A survival counter falling to one in a hundred. REPORTED: standard crocodilian biology references.

There is another side to this animal.

A female builds a nest of mud and plants, and lays around fifty eggs.

The heat inside the nest decides whether each one becomes male or female.

She guards it for about three months. She barely leaves.

When the babies call from inside the eggs, she digs them out.

Then she picks them up in the same jaws that can crush a skull, and carries them gently to the water.

Even so, about one in a hundred lives to grow up.

The one watching you is that one in a hundred.

## 15. What it does when it sees you first

**Visual:** The sequence as a numbered scan: watch, learn, sink, approach, strike. A footprint trail returning to the same spot on a bank, day after day, with the animal's position moving closer. REPORTED: Northern Territory Government guidance.

So here is what really happens when it sees you first.

It does not charge. It studies.

It watches how close you come to the water. How long you stay. Whether you come back.

Rangers warn about this. Fill your bucket at the same spot each morning, and one morning it will be waiting at that spot.

Then it sinks. It comes in along the bottom, where you cannot see it.

It stops one body length away.

And it waits for the moment you look down.

## 16. How you live

**Visual:** A checklist, one line at a time, each with its own small diagram: the five-metre line, the fifty-metre camp, the bucket, the sign. Then the last resort: targets on the eyes and snout. LOGGED: Northern Territory and Queensland governments; REPORTED: survivor accounts.

You survive a crocodile before you ever meet it.

Stay five metres back from the water's edge.

Camp at least fifty metres from the water.

Never use the same spot twice.

And if a sign says crocodiles live here, believe the sign. Even if the water looks empty. Especially if it looks empty.

If the worst happens, do not play dead. Fight.

Go for the eyes. Hit the snout. Make yourself more trouble than you are worth.

Survivors say the same thing. It let go because they did not stop.

## 17. Close

**Visual:** Back through the mask. The flat brown water. The two bumps rise again, closer. You back out onto the bank. The instruments show your heart rate falling. End card.

So you do the only smart thing left.

You stop walking forward. You back up, slowly, the way you came.

Out of the water. Up the bank. Five metres. Ten.

Behind you, the surface is flat again.

Then two small bumps rise, exactly where you were standing.

It was that close. You never felt a thing.

It saw you first. This time, you saw it second. And that was just enough.
