## 1. Cold open

**Visual:** No title. A yellow kayak on grey water, mountains behind. A dark shape rising under it. The water bulges, the kayak tips up, black. Then flashes: a diver in a dark mouth, a seal on a whale's chest, an orca fin. Title at about 12 s, then the three promises one by one.

February 2025. The Strait of Magellan, at the bottom of South America.

A young man named Adrián is paddling a yellow kayak. His father is a few metres behind him, filming.

The water under Adrián turns dark.

And then, in one second, Adrián and his kayak are gone. Inside the mouth of a humpback whale.

So what actually happens when a humpback whale swallows you? Can it really swallow you? How long could you last in there? And why does this same whale risk its life to save other animals from killers?

## 2. Two seconds

**Visual:** The father's view: the kayak, then the empty spot. A dive-time counter in the HUD ticking 0:01, 0:02. The pulse readout jumping. Adrián bursting up, his father's kayak, a rope of words: "GRAB THE BOAT". Sources: AP, CNN (verified video), NPR 14 Feb 2025.

For a moment there is nothing. Just a swirl of water where a person used to be.

Adrián said later he thought he was dead. He felt something slimy close over his face, and everything went dark.

Then, just as fast, the whale opened up and let him go.

He popped up in the cold water, gasping. His father kept filming, and kept calling to him. Stay calm. Grab the boat.

Adrián reached his father's kayak. Both of them made it back to shore without a scratch.

The whole thing lasted a few seconds. But to understand what really happened in those seconds, you need to know what grabbed him.

## 3. The animal

**Visual:** Scale shot: a humpback length bar growing past a person, a bus, a second bus. A weight counter to 40 tonnes. The long white flippers drawn out. Then the knobbly head with the bumps lighting up. Sources: NOAA Fisheries; NSW Government.

This is the humpback whale.

An adult is fourteen to seventeen metres long. About as long as a city bus, and then some.

It can weigh up to forty tonnes. That is heavier than six elephants. [CHECK: African elephant about 6 t]

Its flippers are the longest of any whale. Each one is up to five metres. A third of its body. [CHECK: NOAA]

Those bumps on its head are not barnacles. Each one holds a single hair, like a whisker. [CHECK]

And it has no teeth at all. Not one.

So if it cannot bite, what is it doing with a mouth that big?

## 4. The mouth

**Visual:** Side cutaway: the jaw drops open almost ninety degrees, the pleated throat stretching like a balloon. Krill drawn as a pink cloud. Baleen plates as a comb. Water pushing out through the comb, krill staying in. Sources: NOAA; Goldbogen et al. on lunge feeding.

Instead of teeth, it has baleen. Hundreds of plates hanging from the top of its mouth, like the teeth of a giant comb. [CHECK: about 270–400 plates per side]

It hunts tiny things. Krill, small shrimp-like animals. And small fish.

To catch them, it charges at a swarm and drops its jaw open.

Its throat is folded in long pleats, like an accordion. As water rushes in, the pleats stretch, and the throat balloons out.

In a single gulp, it can take in tens of thousands of litres of water. [CHECK: Goldbogen; humpback engulfment volume]

Then it pushes the water out through the comb. The food stays in.

It does this without looking at each fish. It just swallows the whole patch. And sometimes, there is something else in that patch.

## 5. Lunge feeding

**Visual:** A swarm of fish near the surface. The whale drawn rising from below, mouth open, breaking the surface. Seabirds diving at the same spot. Then the kayak drawn in the same spot. Source: Vanessa Pirotta, Macquarie University, via NPR.

This is called lunge feeding.

The whale picks a spot, rises fast from below, and opens its mouth at the surface.

From above, you get almost no warning. The water goes dark, and maybe it starts to boil with fish.

Scientists who studied Adrián's video think that is exactly what happened. The whale was lunging at a school of fish. Adrián was sitting right on top of it.

It never meant to take him. It did not even know he was there until he was in its mouth.

And the moment it felt a hard plastic kayak, it spat everything out.

But there is one man who was not so lucky. He did not get a few seconds. He got much longer.

## 6. Cape Cod, 2021

**Visual:** Map: Cape Cod, a pin on Provincetown. Date stamp 11 June 2021. A diver at 13 m with lobster bag, sunlight shafts. A sudden truck-like hit drawn as a shock ring. Black. The HUD depth reading 13 m, air gauge. Sources: NPR, CBS, WBUR 11–12 June 2021; Michael Packard's own Facebook account; Center for Coastal Studies.

June 2021. Off the coast of Cape Cod, in Massachusetts.

Michael Packard is a lobster diver. He has done this for decades. He is about thirteen metres down, near the bottom.

Then something hits him. He said it felt like being run over by a truck.

Everything went black.

At first he thought it was a shark. Then he realised there were no teeth. And he could feel the whale's muscles squeezing around him.

He was inside a humpback whale's mouth. With his air regulator still in.

He thought about his wife and his two sons. He was sure he was going to die.

## 7. Inside

**Visual:** Dark, POV inside the mouth: only the diver's torch, soft pink walls, the baleen fringe as a curtain. HUD: dive time counting up, air dropping, pulse at 160. Then the whale tilting, light flooding in. Packard tumbling out on the surface, a boat. Source: Packard's account; Cape Cod Hospital release.

So what was it like in there?

Dark. Tight. Wet. And moving. The whale was swimming, and it was shaking its head.

He could still breathe from his tank. That was the only reason he had time to think at all.

Then the whale rose to the surface, opened its mouth, and threw him out into the air.

He landed in the water and floated there. His crewmate pulled him into the boat.

He was in the whale's mouth for around thirty seconds. Maybe forty.

He went to hospital with bruises all over his body. No broken bones. He went home the same day.

So he was in the mouth of a whale. But was he ever really in danger of being swallowed?

## 8. Could it swallow you?

**Visual:** Cutaway of the head: the huge mouth, then a narrow red tube leading back, the throat. A fist drawn beside it, same size. The throat stretching to a dinner plate, a human outline next to it, not fitting. Large MYTH stamp on "swallowed by a whale". Sources: National Geographic; Whale and Dolphin Conservation (Nicola Hodgins).

Here is the answer to the first question.

A humpback's mouth is big enough to fit a car. But its throat is not.

The opening to its stomach is about the size of your fist.

Even fully stretched, it only reaches around thirty to thirty-eight centimetres. Smaller than your shoulders.

It can take you in. It cannot swallow you. Its body would simply not let you through.

Scientists have found seabirds in humpback whale droppings. Small things can slip down by accident. A person cannot.

So in the strict sense, no humpback has ever swallowed a person.

But there is one story, more than a hundred years old, that says a whale did.

## 9. The man in the whale

**Visual:** Old-film grade: a 19th-century whaling ship, harpoon lines, a sailor overboard. A newspaper page drawn with a headline counting up to the year 1891. Then a red MYTH stamp. Source: Edward B. Davis, "A Whale of a Tale", 1991. [CHECK]

In 1891, newspapers told the story of a sailor named James Bartley.

His ship, the Star of the East, was hunting a whale near the Falkland Islands. The whale smashed a boat, and Bartley disappeared.

The next day, the crew killed a whale and cut it open. And inside, they said, was Bartley. Alive. His skin bleached white by the stomach acid.

The story went around the world. It is still repeated today.

But when a historian checked it a hundred years later, almost nothing fit. The captain's wife wrote that no man had ever been lost overboard on her husband's ship. [CHECK]

And that whale was said to be a sperm whale. The one whale that actually could swallow a person.

That is the myth. The real humpback is very different.

## 10. Bubble nets

**Visual:** Top-down view: a ring of bubbles drawn spiralling up from below, a school of fish trapped in the middle, then several whale mouths breaking the surface together. A second whale calling, a sound-wave line. Source: NOAA; Alaska Fisheries Science Center. [CHECK]

Because humpbacks are not just big. They are clever.

In Alaska, groups of them hunt together using a trick no other whale is known to use the same way.

One whale dives below a school of fish and swims in a circle, blowing a stream of bubbles.

The bubbles rise like a curtain, and the fish are too scared to swim through them.

The circle gets smaller. The fish are packed tighter and tighter.

Then the whales rise up through the middle, all at once, mouths open.

This is a bubble net. And if you were in a boat above it, you would see a ring of bubbles appear on calm water, a few seconds before the whales come through.

That ring is a warning. Most people do not know how to read it.

## 11. The song

**Visual:** A male whale hanging head-down in blue water, flippers out. A spectrogram line drawn scrolling, a timer counting to 20:00. A map of the Pacific with the song spreading as coloured waves from Australia to the east. Sources: NOAA; Garland et al., Current Biology, 2011. [CHECK]

The males have another skill. They sing.

They hang in the water, almost still, and make long, haunting sounds. Moans, groans, and high squeals.

One song can last up to twenty minutes. Then they sing it again. For hours.

Every male in the same area sings the same song. But the song keeps changing.

In the South Pacific, scientists watched new songs spread from whale to whale, from Australia eastwards across the ocean, over a few years. [CHECK: Garland 2011]

No one knows exactly why they sing. To attract a female, perhaps. Or to warn other males.

Either way, for weeks of the year, the ocean is full of their music. Which makes what we did to them even harder to hear.

## 12. We nearly silenced them

**Visual:** Old-film grade: a steam whaling ship, a harpoon cannon, a whale on a slipway. A population line falling steeply, a "PROTECTED" marker, then the line rising. One whale drawn alone in empty water, then many. Source: NOAA Fisheries. [CHECK]

For about a century, humpbacks were hunted almost everywhere they swam.

They were slow. They swam close to the coast. And they floated when they were killed. That made them easy to take.

By the time commercial whaling was finally stopped in 1985, most humpback populations had been cut by more than ninety-five percent.

For every hundred whales, fewer than five were left.

Then people stopped. And something remarkable happened.

The humpbacks came back. In many oceans, there are now tens of thousands again. [CHECK: IUCN Least Concern]

Which means more whales are feeding where people kayak, dive, and swim. And that is why encounters like Adrián's are being filmed more than ever.

## 13. The longest journey

**Visual:** Map of the Pacific: a tagged whale's path from Costa Rica down to Antarctica, a distance counter to 8,300 km and a day counter. Then the warm water drawn with a mother and calf; cold water drawn with a krill cloud. Source: NOAA; Rasmussen et al., Biology Letters, 2007. [CHECK]

And those whales travel further than almost anything alive.

Every year, they swim from cold feeding waters near the poles to warm, calm water near the tropics, where they give birth.

Some humpbacks swim more than eight thousand kilometres, one way. One of the longest migrations of any mammal on Earth.

On the warm-water side, there is almost nothing for them to eat. So for months, the mothers live off their fat. And feed a growing calf.

Then they swim all the way back.

So the whale that grabbed Adrián was hungry, in its feeding grounds, after a journey like that.

But the strangest thing about humpbacks is not how they eat. It is who they protect.

## 14. The rescue

**Visual:** Antarctica, ice floe. A Weddell seal drawn on a floe, orcas circling and washing it off with a wave. A humpback rising beneath it, rolling on its back, the seal on its chest. The seal sliding back onto the ice. Source: Pitman et al., Marine Mammal Science, 2016; Pitman's own account (2009).

In 2009, in Antarctica, a scientist named Robert Pitman was watching a pack of killer whales hunt a seal.

The seal was on a small piece of ice. The orcas swam at it together, making waves to wash it off.

Then two humpback whales arrived.

The seal slid into the water, right toward them. And one humpback rolled onto its back, and lifted the seal up onto its chest, out of the water, between its huge flippers.

When the seal started to slide off, the whale nudged it back up with a flipper.

Then the seal swam to another piece of ice, and got away.

Pitman could not believe it. So he started collecting other reports. And what he found was even stranger.

## 15. Why it fights killers

**Visual:** A grid of drawn icons filling one by one: seal, sea lion, gray whale calf, sunfish. A counter to 115. Then a humpback charging an orca pack, flippers swinging, the barnacles drawn as weapons. Answer to promise 3 shown on screen. Source: Pitman et al., 2016.

He found more than a hundred reports of humpbacks and killer whales meeting.

In most of them, the humpbacks were charging in to break up an attack.

And most of the time, the animal being attacked was not a humpback. It was a seal. A sea lion. A grey whale calf. Even a sunfish.

Humpbacks are one of the only whales that fight back against orcas. Their flippers are covered in sharp barnacles, and one swing can hurt.

So why save a seal?

Scientists think it may begin with protecting their own calves. They hear orcas attacking and they go, without checking who the victim is.

Whatever the reason, it means one of the biggest animals in the sea spends energy saving animals it does not need. It does not eat them. It will not mate with them.

Which makes you wonder what it thought, when it found a person in its mouth.

## 16. If it happens to you

**Visual:** A checklist with a small drawing for each line: seabirds diving, a ring of bubbles, the 100 m line from a boat, a diver with a surface buoy. Then a calm scene of a kayak far from a feeding whale. Sources: NOAA marine-life viewing guidelines; local rules vary. [CHECK]

So how do you make sure this never happens to you?

Watch for the signs of feeding. Seabirds diving into one spot. Fish jumping out of the water. A ring of bubbles.

If you see them, paddle away from that spot. Do not paddle toward it to get a better look.

Keep your distance. In many countries the rule for boats is about a hundred metres from a whale. [CHECK]

If you dive in whale waters, use a surface buoy so boats and whales can see where you are.

And if the worst happens and you end up in its mouth? Both survivors did the same thing: they stayed as calm as they could, and waited. [CHECK: no official guidance on this] The whale wants you out even more than you want to leave.

## 17. Close

**Visual:** Back to the Strait of Magellan. The yellow kayak floating, calm water, mountains. The dark shape passing far underneath, the tail rising and sliding under. HUD pulse falling back to normal. End card.

So what happens when a humpback whale swallows you?

It does not. It cannot.

For a few seconds, maybe half a minute, you are inside the mouth of one of the gentlest giants on Earth. In the dark. Squeezed. Sure that you are about to die.

And then it does the one thing it was always going to do.

It opens its mouth, and lets you go.

The same animal that throws a person back into the sea will lift a seal out of it.

It was never hunting you. It just did not see you. This time, it was a mistake. And it fixed it.
