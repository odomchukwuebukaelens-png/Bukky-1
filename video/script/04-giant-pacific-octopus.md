# What Happens When a Giant Pacific Octopus Grabs You

Draft 2. Target length: 15 minutes. The test clip in the "Deep sea" voice ran at about 171 words per minute, so the narration is sized for that pace.
Thumbnail text: IT STUDIES YOU

Each scene has narration (what the voice says) and a visual note (what the animation shows).
Lines marked [CHECK] rest on commonly quoted figures or stories that still need a primary source before publishing.

---

## 1. Hook

**Visual:** Cold green water, a rocky wall, a diver's torch beam. A single red arm uncurls from a crack and touches the diver's glove.

The water here is cold, green, and dark. You are twenty metres down beside a wall of rock, and your torch only reaches a few metres ahead.

There is a crack in the wall. In front of it, a neat pile of empty crab shells.

You lean in to look. And something leans out to look at you.

One arm. Thicker than your wrist, the colour of rust. It reaches across the gap, slowly, and lays itself along your glove. The suckers flex. Each one is pressing, pulling, tasting.

It is not attacking you. It is doing something much stranger.

It is finding out what you are.

So what happens if it decides to keep you?

## 2. The animal

**Visual:** The octopus slides fully out of its den and spreads its arms beside the diver for scale. A map of the North Pacific rim lights up.

This is the giant Pacific octopus. The largest octopus species on Earth.

It lives around the cold rim of the North Pacific, from California up through Alaska and across to Japan.

A typical adult weighs somewhere between fifteen and fifty kilograms, with arms that stretch three to five metres from tip to tip. Wide enough to reach from one side of a small room to the other.

There are old reports of animals far bigger than that. Nine metres across. More than two hundred and seventy kilograms. Those records are disputed, and nobody has measured one like it in modern times.

It does not need to be that big. The ordinary ones are already longer than you are.

## 3. The den

**Visual:** A cross-section of the rock wall showing a snug cave. Outside it, the heap of shells. Day turns to night and the octopus slides out.

Start with where you found it.

A giant Pacific octopus lives alone. It picks a den, a cave under a boulder or a crack in a wall, with an entrance barely wide enough for its body, and it may stay there for weeks.

The pile of shells outside is the giveaway. Divers call it a midden. It is every meal the octopus has carried home, cracked open, and thrown out the front door.

By day, it mostly stays inside, with one eye at the entrance. At night it goes out to hunt, crawling across the seabed, then finds its way back to the same den in the dark.

So you did not stumble on it by accident. You knocked on its door.

## 4. No bones

**Visual:** The octopus pours itself through a hole the size of a lemon. An x-ray view shows the only hard part, the beak, passing through last.

Now look at how it came out of that crack. It did not climb. It poured.

An octopus has no skeleton. No bones, no shell, nothing rigid at all, except one thing: a beak, hidden in the middle of its arms.

That beak sets the limit. If the beak fits through a gap, the rest of the animal can follow. A forty-kilogram octopus can pass through a hole not much wider than a lemon.

This is why aquariums that keep them lock the lids.

And it matters for you. You cannot pin it, and you cannot hold it. There is nothing solid to hold.

## 5. The arms

**Visual:** Close-up of an arm. A counter runs up as suckers are highlighted. One sucker lifts a weight labelled 16 kg.

Each of its eight arms carries two rows of suckers. Around two hundred and eighty of them on a single arm. More than two thousand on the whole animal.

They are not simple suction cups. Each one can move on its own, grip, turn, and pass an object along to the next, like a line of fingers.

The biggest are about as wide as a golf ball, and a single one of those can hold roughly sixteen kilograms.

Now multiply that.

But grip is only half of what they do. The suckers are also covered in sensors that detect chemicals. When that arm rested on your glove, it was not just holding you.

It was tasting you.

## 6. Nine brains, sort of

**Visual:** A diagram of the nervous system. The central brain glows, then eight thicker cords light up along the arms. One arm explores a crevice while the eyes look elsewhere.

People say an octopus has nine brains. That is not quite right, but the truth is close.

It has one central brain, wrapped around its throat. And it has roughly five hundred million nerve cells in total, about as many as a dog. [CHECK]

Here is the strange part. Most of those nerve cells are not in the brain. They are in the arms.

Each arm has its own cluster of nerves, and can make simple decisions by itself. An arm can explore a crack, find a crab, and grab it, while the rest of the animal is busy watching you.

So when one arm takes hold of your wrist, you are not dealing with a single mind giving a single order.

You are dealing with something closer to a committee.

## 7. Three hearts, blue blood

**Visual:** Cutaway of the body: three hearts pulsing, blood drawn in blue. When the octopus jets away, the central heart pauses.

Inside its body are three hearts.

Two of them push blood through the gills. The third pumps it around the rest of the body.

And that blood is blue. Ours carries oxygen with iron, which makes it red. An octopus uses copper, which works better in cold, low-oxygen water, and turns the blood blue.

There is a catch. This system is not built for stamina. An octopus that swims hard tires quickly. That is why it prefers to crawl.

Remember that. It is strong, but it is not built for a long fight.

## 8. The skin

**Visual:** The octopus crosses rock, sand and kelp, changing colour and texture to match each one in under a second. Then it flashes dark red.

Then there is the skin.

It is packed with tiny sacs of pigment, each one wired to a nerve. By squeezing and stretching them, the octopus can change colour faster than you can blink.

It can change texture too. Smooth one moment, covered in spikes and ridges the next, to match rock or weed.

And here is the puzzle. As far as we can tell, octopuses are colour-blind. Their eyes have only one kind of light sensor. Yet they match colours they should not be able to see. Scientists are still working out how.

For a diver, the skin is also a warning. Pale and smooth means calm. Dark red, with raised skin, means it has made up its mind about something.

## 9. The escape kit

**Visual:** A seal lunges. The octopus fires a cloud of ink, jets backwards, and vanishes. Then a close-up of a short arm slowly growing back.

Disguise is its first defence. It has others.

If something finds it anyway, it fires a cloud of dark ink. The ink hangs in the water in a blob about the size of the octopus itself, a decoy for the attacker to bite at, and it dulls a predator's sense of smell.

Behind that cloud, the octopus is already gone. It sucks water into its body and blasts it out through a funnel, shooting backwards like a rocket.

And if a predator does get hold of an arm, the octopus can afford to lose it. The arm grows back, complete with suckers and nerves.

Everything about this animal is built for getting out of trouble. Remember that too. Most of the time, getting away from you is exactly what it wants.

## 10. The mouth

**Visual:** The underside of the octopus: arms part to show the black beak. A cutaway shows a crab shell being drilled, then the crab going limp.

At the centre of all those arms is the mouth.

The beak looks like a parrot's, and it is made of the same sort of tough material as your fingernails. It can crack a crab.

Behind it is a tongue covered in tiny teeth. When a shell is too thick to crack, the octopus drills a neat hole through it, and injects saliva that paralyses the animal inside and begins to dissolve it.

Yes, that means it is venomous.

To a crab, that is the end. To you, a bite would hurt, swell, and bleed, but its venom is not known to be dangerous to people.

The bite is not what you should be worried about.

## 11. How it hunts

**Visual:** Night. The octopus creeps over the seabed, then launches itself and spreads the webbing between its arms like a parachute over a crab.

Watch it hunt and you see all of those tools working together.

It crawls across the bottom, arms spread, each one feeling into holes and under rocks. When an arm touches something that tastes like food, it grabs.

For anything in the open, it uses a different move. It rises up, spreads the skin between its arms into a wide tent, and drops over its prey like a parachute. Whatever is underneath has nowhere to go.

It can hold several crabs at once, tucked under its body, and carry them all back to the den to eat in safety.

Then the beak and the drill go to work.

## 12. What it eats

**Visual:** A sequence of prey silhouettes getting bigger: crab, clam, fish, small shark. Then a seagull on a rock at the waterline, and an arm rising behind it.

Mostly it eats crabs, clams, and fish. The pile of shells outside its den is its rubbish heap.

But it is not limited to small things.

In 2012, a woman walking along a breakwater in Victoria, Canada, photographed something nobody had recorded before. A giant Pacific octopus reached out of the water, wrapped a seagull, and pulled it under. It was over in about a minute.

And at the Seattle Aquarium, staff once kept finding dead sharks in a large tank. They were small sharks, about a metre long. When they set up a camera, they found the culprit. The octopus was catching them at night. [CHECK]

So it eats birds. And it eats sharks.

You are much bigger than either. But it is worth knowing what it is capable of.

## 13. What hunts it

**Visual:** A harbour seal shaking an octopus at the surface. Then thousands of tiny hatchlings drifting among plankton, most blinking out one by one.

For all of that, the octopus is not at the top.

Seals and sea lions eat them. So do sea otters, large sharks, and big fish like halibut. A seal will drag one to the surface and shake it apart.

And the young have it far worse. A newly hatched giant Pacific octopus is the size of a grain of rice. It spends its first months drifting near the surface, where almost everything eats it.

Out of tens of thousands of hatchlings, only a handful ever reach the seabed and grow up.

So the animal in front of you is not just any octopus. It is one of the very few that made it.

## 14. It studies you

**Visual:** Two identical figures in the same blue shirt stand at a tank. The octopus turns pale and reaches for one, turns dark and squirts water at the other.

Here is where it stops being just a predator.

In Seattle, researchers ran an experiment with eight giant Pacific octopuses and two people. Both people wore the same uniform. One of them always brought food. The other always poked the octopus with a bristly stick.

Within two weeks, the octopuses could tell them apart.

When the kind one came near, they reached towards them. When the other one appeared, they pulled back, changed colour, and aimed their water jets at them.

They were not recognising a uniform. They were recognising a person.

So that arm on your glove is not just checking whether you are food. It is building a file on you.

## 15. It plays

**Visual:** An empty pill bottle floats in a tank. The octopus blows it with its water jet; the current carries it back; it blows it again.

The same scientists tried something else. They gave octopuses an empty plastic bottle, floating in the tank, and watched what happened.

Most of them checked it, found it was not food, and lost interest.

But two of them did something no one expected. They blew jets of water at the bottle, sending it across the tank and into the current, which carried it back. Then they did it again. And again.

One of them kept it going like a bouncing ball.

It served no purpose. It was not food and it was not shelter. As far as anyone could tell, it was play. It was the first time play had been reported in an animal without a backbone.

So the thing holding your wrist is strong, armed, curious, and possibly bored.

## 16. So what happens when it grabs you?

**Visual:** The octopus has two arms on the diver. One arm drifts up toward the mask and the regulator hose. A warning marker appears on the breathing gear.

Now back to you, and the arm on your glove.

In almost every encounter, this is what happens next. It feels you. It tastes the rubber of your suit. It finds you are not a crab. And it lets go.

Sometimes it does not let go straight away. Divers have had a camera pulled from their hands, a mask tugged, a second arm wrapped around a leg.

There is no confirmed case of a giant Pacific octopus killing a person.

But the risk is real, and it is simple. You are breathing through a hose. The octopus is curious about anything that sticks out, and it is strong enough to pull that hose from your mouth, or the mask from your face, at twenty metres down.

That is the danger. Not the beak. Not the venom. The fact that you need your equipment and it wants to find out what it is.

Divers who know these animals give the same advice. Stay calm. Protect your mask and your air. Do not yank. Peel the arms off gently, from the tips, and back away. It tires faster than you do, and it has no reason to hold on.

## 17. We grabbed them first

**Visual:** A 1960s beach scene: a crowd, divers hauling an octopus ashore, a scoreboard. Then a modern sign marking a protected dive site.

There is an awkward fact in this story. For a long time, we were the ones doing the grabbing.

In the 1960s, in Washington State, there was a sport called octopus wrestling. Divers went down, pulled giant Pacific octopuses from their dens, and dragged them to the surface to be weighed. Thousands of people came to watch. [CHECK]

The sport faded. But in 2012, a diver in Seattle legally caught and killed one at a popular dive site, in front of other divers. The anger that followed led the state to protect octopuses at several of those sites. [CHECK]

The more we learned about what these animals are, the harder it became to treat them as a trophy.

## 18. The short life

**Visual:** A den with strings of white eggs hanging from the roof. The mother, pale and thin, blows water over them. Hatchlings the size of rice grains drift up and away.

And there is one more thing you should know about the animal holding your hand.

It is probably only three or four years old. And it does not have long.

A giant Pacific octopus lives for three to five years. It hatches smaller than a grain of rice, and in that short time it becomes everything you have just seen.

Then it breeds once.

A female lays tens of thousands of eggs, in strings, on the roof of her den. For the next six months or so she does not leave. She cleans them. She blows fresh water over them. She does not eat. [CHECK]

When they hatch, she dies.

All that intelligence, built from nothing in three years, and never passed on. Every octopus has to learn the world alone.

## 19. Close

**Visual:** Back to the diver and the wall. The arm slides off the glove and curls back into the crack. An eye watches from the dark, then closes.

So what happens when a giant Pacific octopus grabs you?

It learns what you feel like. What you taste like. Whether you are dangerous.

Then the suckers release, one at a time. The arm slides back across the gap and folds into the rock.

The last thing you see is an eye, watching you from the dark.

You will swim away with a story.

And for the rest of its short life, it will remember you.
